\section{Problem statement}
\label{sec:problem}

LVM \citep{Drory2024} observes extended, low-surface-brightness emission from
the Milky Way and the Magellanic Clouds, and for such targets the night sky
is a first-order contaminant. Alongside each science exposure, two dedicated
sky telescopes observe blank fields several degrees or more away. On that
baseline the sky is not uniform. Scattered moonlight has strong angular
structure, set by the scattering phase functions of molecules and aerosols; the zodiacal light
changes by tens of percent with ecliptic latitude and solar elongation
across the pointings of one night; and mesospheric gravity waves modulate the
OH airglow on scales of tens of arcminutes. The three lines of sight also
have different zenith angles, so each crosses the emitting layers and the
atmosphere along a different slant path. Subtracting either sky spectrum
directly leaves systematic residuals of one to ten percent of the sky, enough
to bury the diffuse emission the survey is after.

LVM has four 16.1\,cm siderostat-fed telescopes on one platform. Sci feeds the
science integral-field unit (IFU), SkyE and SkyW feed two small sky IFUs, and
Spec observes spectrophotometric standard stars; SkyE and SkyW are named for
their place on the platform, not for where they point. The IFUs are
hexagonal arrays of circular spaxels $35.3''$ across at a $37''$ pitch, each
with its own fiber: 1801 for the science IFU, which covers $0.165$\,deg$^2$,
59 and 60 for the sky IFUs, and 24 for the standards. The 1944 fibers are
sorted into three pseudo-slits of 648 fibers, one per spectrograph, in blocks
of 36 whose first and last fibers are sky fibers, which samples the sky along
the whole slit. Each spectrograph covers $3600$--$9800$\,\AA{} in three
channels ($b$, $r$, $z$) at resolving powers of about 2900, 4700 and 4900.

A science observation is a single 900\,s exposure of a tile, or nine dithered
exposures for the Magellanic Clouds and nearby galaxies; Galactic tiles are
observed at any lunar phase if they are more than $60^\circ$ from the Moon.
During the exposure SkyE and SkyW observe two sky fields that the scheduler
selects for the tile and the time, and Spec observes 12 standards in turn. One
field is the nearest ``WHAM Darkest'' field, a region of extremely faint
H$\alpha$ ($<2.8\times10^{-18}$\,erg\,s$^{-1}$\,cm$^{-2}$\,arcsec$^{-2}$ in the
Wisconsin H$\alpha$ Mapper) at Galactic latitude $|b|>30^\circ$, and is often
far from the tile. The other is the nearest of 768 ``Gridded'' fields, one per
element of a HEALPix tessellation with a mean separation of $7.3^\circ$, which
are nearer but less clean. The two fields are assigned to SkyE and SkyW at
random. Every science spectrum thus comes with two simultaneous sky spectra
from lines of sight at different angular distances, airmasses and angles from
the Moon, and neither field is entirely free of Galactic emission.

We therefore replace direct subtraction by a prediction. We first decompose
each spectrum into the contributions of physically distinct emitters
(Section~\ref{sec:decomp}) and convert these to a zenith-equivalent frame
that removes the known effect of viewing geometry
(Section~\ref{sec:geometry}). A neural network, which we call the
\emph{transfer}, then predicts the contributions at the science pointing from
those at the two sky pointings and from the observing geometry
(Section~\ref{sec:ml}). We turn the prediction back into a sky spectrum on
the native pixel grid and subtract it from the science spectrum.

Because the sky model is subtracted from individual fibers, we measure the
result against the photon noise of a single fiber. On observations not used
in training, the predicted sky reaches a median reduced $\chi^2$ of 1.12
against that noise, while the decomposition's own fit to the same spectra
reaches 0.77. The prediction thus adds an error of about 0.6 times the
single-fiber noise, or about 5\% of the sky per pixel
(Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}). Two empirical corrections, measured on the
same exposure, lower the median to 0.31, below the decomposition's own fit
(Section~\ref{sec:postcorr}).

\section{Input data}
\label{sec:inputs}

\paragraph{Instrument.} LVM has separate telescopes for the science field,
for two sky fields (east and west) and for spectrophotometric standards. All
of them feed fiber bundles into spectrographs with three channels ($b$, $r$,
$z$) that together cover $3600$--$9800$\,\AA; the fibers are $35.3''$
across. We call the three pointings of an exposure its \emph{arms}: the
science arm and two sky arms. The sky arm closer to the science field is the
\emph{near} arm and the other the \emph{far} arm, so the assignment changes
from exposure to exposure and does not depend on which telescope took the
spectrum.

\paragraph{Spectra.} We start from the reduced, flux-calibrated frames of the
LVM data reduction pipeline (DRP). The DRP divides every fiber by a model
telluric transmission evaluated along the \emph{science} line of sight, which
the decomposition has to account for (Section~\ref{sec:telluric}). For each
exposure we form three spectra on a common grid of $N_\lambda = 12\,401$
pixels at $\Delta\lambda = 0.5$\,\AA: the median over the fibers of each sky
telescope, and the median over the science fibers once we have rejected
fibers whose Gaia DR3 \citep{GaiaDR3} predicted stellar $G$-band flux exceeds
1\% of the fiber's measured flux plus sky. The median gives a robust estimate
of the sky-dominated spectrum, with the variance of a mean over
$N_{\rm eff} = (2/\pi)\,n_{\rm fib}$ fibers, where $n_{\rm fib}$ is the
number of fibers combined. Each spectrum carries the per-pixel line-spread
function (LSF) width of its fiber bundle and a record of which physical fibers
entered its median, from which we form its reference LSF
(Section~\ref{sec:lsf}).

\paragraph{Metadata.} Each exposure provides the pointing coordinates and
airmass of all three telescopes, the exposure mid-time and duration, the
precipitable water vapour (PWV) used by the DRP, and which sky telescope is
near and which far. From these we compute the solar, lunar and ecliptic
geometry of each pointing, and we look up solar and geomagnetic activity
indices for the date (Section~\ref{sec:ctx}).

\paragraph{Data set.} The data set contains $18\,668$ exposures. We call one
exposure with its three spectra (science, near, far) an \emph{observation};
$13\,688$ observations pass the selection of Section~\ref{sec:selection}.

\section{Physical decomposition}
\label{sec:decomp}

We fit each of the three spectra of an observation independently with the
same physical model. The fit returns a non-negative vector of $P = 388$
coefficients, one per template spectrum, together with their uncertainties,
the spectrum of each emitter and a set of reliability flags. The transfer of
Section~\ref{sec:ml} works on these coefficients, so we designed the
decomposition to give each coefficient a fixed physical meaning from one
observation to the next, even where the data alone could not enforce it.

\subsection{Model}
\label{sec:model}

We write the observed spectrum as a non-negative linear combination of
templates:
\begin{equation}
  y(\lambda) \;\simeq\; \sum_{k=1}^{P} c_k\,T_k(\lambda), \qquad c_k \ge 0 ,
  \label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
where each template $T_k$ is a physical spectrum convolved with the LSF
of the same spectrum (Section~\ref{sec:lsf}), attenuated by the
atmosphere as appropriate for its emitting layer
(Section~\ref{sec:telluric}), and divided by the transmission the DRP
removed. Stacked, the templates form the design matrix
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  \mathbf{A} = \big[\,&\mathbf{A}_{\rm OH}\,\big|\,\mathbf{A}_{\rm O_2}\,\big|\,
  \mathbf{A}_{\rm atom}\,\big|\\
  &\mathbf{A}_{\rm moon}\,\big|\,\mathbf{A}_{\rm zodi}\,\big|\,
  \mathbf{A}_{\rm diff}\,\big]
  \in \mathbb{R}^{N_\lambda\times P}.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
We group the templates into \emph{families}, one per emitter
(Table~\ref{tab:families}). The families fall into a \emph{line} block (OH,
O$_2$, atomic lines), which depends on the LSF, and a \emph{continuum} block
(moonlight, zodiacal light, diffuse airglow), which is smooth on the scale
of the LSF; the estimation (Section~\ref{sec:estimation}) is organised
around this division.

We define the \emph{amplitude} of a family $g$ as the flux it contributes,
integrated over wavelength,
\begin{equation}
  A_g = \vect{c}_g\cdot\vect{v}_g ,
  \label{eq:amplitude}
\end{equation}
where $\vect c_g$ holds the family's coefficients and $\vect v_g$ the
wavelength integrals of its templates. We take the integrals before
atmospheric attenuation (Section~\ref{sec:telluric}), so $A_g$ measures the
emission itself. For moonlight and zodiacal light the coefficients are
spline weights that also set the spectral shape, and the amplitude is their
total flux.

\subsection{Line-spread function}
\label{sec:lsf}

Every narrow template depends on the LSF, which varies with wavelength,
between spectrograph channels and from fiber to fiber. We describe the LSF
of channel $c\in\{b,r,z\}$ as a continuous probability density in the offset
$\delta = \lambda' - \lambda$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  K_c(\delta;\lambda) &= \sum_{j=1}^{11}\sum_{m=1}^{4}
  \theta_{c,jm}\,M_j(\delta)\,B_m(\lambda),\\
  \theta_{c,jm}&\ge0,\qquad \sum_j \theta_{c,jm} = 1 .
\end{split}
  \label{eq:lsf}
\end{equation}
Here the $M_j$ are cubic M-splines on equally spaced knots over
$|\delta|\le2.5$\,\AA, each normalised to unit integral, and the $B_m$ are
four cubic B-splines in wavelength on uniform knots across the channel. The
$B_m$ are non-negative and sum to one at every wavelength, so $K_c$ is
non-negative and integrates to one everywhere. We also require every column
$\theta_{c,\cdot m}$ to increase up to the central component $j=6$ and to
decrease after it, which makes the kernel unimodal. To render a line
template, we integrate $K_c(\,\cdot\,;\lambda_\ell)$ analytically over each
pixel for every line $\ell$ of the family.

\paragraph{Blue channel: empirical reference.} The $b$ channel has few sky
emission lines, and on dark nights almost no continuum signal to constrain
its LSF, so we do not fit it to the spectrum being decomposed. Instead we
hold it at an empirical reference built separately for every physical fiber.
We measure the reference on 558 bright-Moon exposures (Moon elevation above
$60^\circ$, illuminated fraction above 0.95, Sun below $-18^\circ$), in
which the scattered moonlight carries the solar Fraunhofer spectrum at high
signal to noise. We decompose each fiber of each exposure individually and
fit its $b$-channel LSF jointly to the sky emission lines and to the solar
absorption lines of the moonlight and zodiacal continuum, rendered as
described below. We keep only converged fits, and only fibers whose
Gaia-predicted stellar $G$-band flux is below 10\% of their measured flux
plus sky. For each fiber and channel the reference is a weighted mean of the
coefficient matrices of its accepted exposures, with equal weight per
observing night and a robust down-weighting of discrepant surfaces. Over the
1871 physical fibers this pools $2.6\times10^6$ accepted channel fits. We
use the survey-wide reference, pooled over all calibration epochs of the
sample.

A spectrum that is the median over many fibers
(Section~\ref{sec:inputs}) receives the equal-weight mean of the references
of exactly the fibers that entered its median. Every constraint on $\theta$
in Equation~(\ref{eq:lsf}) is linear, so the admissible coefficients form a
convex set and any weighted mean of valid references is itself a valid LSF.
Because the representation is linear in $\theta$, the mean of the
coefficients is also exactly the mean of the kernels at every wavelength. An
element-wise median of the coefficients would break the normalisation: on
the reference set its column sums depart from one by up to 0.7\%. The
effective LSF of a median of spectra equals the mean LSF only to first order
in the fiber-to-fiber differences, an approximation the stack also makes
for the DRP LSF width it carries.

\paragraph{Red and near-infrared channels: fitted.} In the $r$ and $z$
channels the airglow lines constrain the kernel well, so we fit its
parameters to the spectrum itself (Section~\ref{sec:estimation}), starting
from the reference of the same fibers. The fit carries a second-difference
penalty along the offset direction, scaled to $10^{-2}$ of the mean diagonal
of the data Hessian, and a low-order polynomial nuisance background in each
channel.

\paragraph{Solar continuum in the blue.} The moonlight and zodiacal-light
templates (Sections~\ref{sec:moonfam} and~\ref{sec:zodifam}) carry the full
solar absorption spectrum, which the LSF shapes, so in the $b$ channel we
render them exactly rather than convolve a tabulated template. Each family
is the product of a smooth, absorption-free envelope $E(\lambda)$, equal to
the template divided by the solar spectrum, and the solar spectrum seen
through the LSF,
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  T(\lambda_i) &= E(\lambda_i)\sum_{j,m} G_{i,jm}\,\theta_{b,jm},\\
  G_{i,jm} &= \frac{1}{\Delta_i}\int_{\Delta_i}\!d\lambda
  \int\! I_\odot(\lambda')\,B_m(\lambda')\,M_j(\lambda-\lambda')\,d\lambda' ,
\end{split}
  \label{eq:solar}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta_i$ is pixel $i$ and the integral runs over every sample of the
high-resolution solar spectrum \citep{Meftah2018}, without regridding or
smoothing. The tensor $\mathbf{G}$ does not depend on the spectrum being
fitted, so we compute it once. In the $r$ and $z$ channels we simply convolve
the continuum templates with the fitted LSF, which has little effect there.

\subsection{Atmospheric transmission}
\label{sec:telluric}

The airglow is emitted at 85--300\,km and reaches the telescope through the
whole lower atmosphere, and in the red and near-infrared the OH Meinel bands
overlap the H$_2$O and O$_2$ absorption bands. Fitted with undimmed
templates, the line amplitudes would absorb the transmission. Because the
transmission depends on airmass and water vapour, it would not cancel
between the pointings, and the transfer would have to predict atmospheric
absorption disguised as mesospheric emission. We avoid this in two steps.
We attenuate each line template at its own centre wavelength by the PALACE
molecular transmission, evaluated from high-resolution line optical depths at
the airmass $X_{\rm src}$ of the fiber being fitted,
\begin{equation}
  t_\ell = \exp\!\Big\{-X_{\rm src}\big[\tau_\ell^{\rm dry}
  + (w/w_0)\,\tau_\ell^{\rm H_2O}\big]\Big\},
\end{equation}
where $w$ is the PWV and $w_0 = 2.5$\,mm is the reference water column.
We then divide every template by the smooth transmission
$T_{\rm DRP}(\lambda;\,X_{\rm sci},w)$ that the DRP removed from all fibers
along the science line of sight, so that model and data meet in the frame in
which the data are delivered. The design matrix of a spectrum becomes
$\mathbf{A}^{\rm obs} = \mathbf{A}(X_{\rm src}, w)/T_{\rm
DRP}(\lambda; X_{\rm sci}, w)$, and the coefficients keep their meaning as
top-of-atmosphere amplitudes of the emitting layer.

Throughout the paper, $\chi^2$ denotes the reduced $\chi^2$ of a residual
under the photon-noise model of Section~\ref{sec:noise}, and \emph{the blue}
denotes the range blueward of 6000\,\AA, where the continuum rather than OH
dominates the sky. Including the transmission lowers the decomposition
$\chi^2$ by about 30\% over the full band and leaves the blue, which has no
telluric absorption, essentially unchanged.

\subsection{OH and O$_2$ airglow}
\label{sec:oh}

OH nightglow dominates the sky beyond about 6000\,\AA, and at the LVM
resolution individual rotational transitions are separated. The model
contains 357 OH templates, each the sum of the PALACE \citep{Noll2025}
transitions that share $(v', N', F')$, with relative strengths from a
population model. Grouping by upper state lets the fit follow changes in the
rotational and vibrational populations, which trace mesospheric temperature
and chemistry, with far fewer free parameters than transitions.

\paragraph{Ridge-corrected line strengths.} We correct the relative line
strengths within each group linearly, with a correction fitted on far-arm sky
spectra under ridge regularisation ($\lambda_{\rm ridge} = 0.1$). It mainly
repairs lines in the $r$ channel, where the canonical relative strengths are
about 0.11\,dex too weak. The fit cannot compensate on its own, because those
lines share coefficients with the brighter $z$-channel lines of the same
upper state. On 1000 test observations the correction lowers the
decomposition $\chi^2$ on OH-dominated pixels by factors of 33 in $r$ and 3.6
in $z$, and by 31\% over the full band, and every observation improves. The
systematic residual at the OH lines, stacked over observations, falls from
1.1\% to 0.14\% of the line peak in $r$. The coefficients themselves barely
change (0.8\% at the 90th percentile), and neither do the continuum partition
or the reliability flags. That is also a warning: a reconstruction must use
the same line strengths as the fit, because a mismatch is invisible in the
coefficients and shows up only in the reconstructed spectrum.

\paragraph{O$_2$ atmospheric band.} We first fit the O$_2$ $b$-band near
8645\,\AA{} on its own; the band shape found for each spectrum then enters
the main fit as a single template with one free amplitude.

\subsection{Atomic lines}

Seven atomic features enter as single templates: the mesopause lines
K\,\textsc{i} 7699, Na\,\textsc{i}\,D and [O\,\textsc{i}] 5577, and the
thermospheric (F-region) lines N\,\textsc{i} 5199, [O\,\textsc{i}]
6300/6364 and the O\,\textsc{i} 7774 and 8446 recombination lines. The
distinction matters for the geometric normalisation
(Section~\ref{sec:heights}).

\subsection{Scattered moonlight}
\label{sec:moonfam}

With the Moon up, scattered moonlight is the dominant broadband contaminant.
Its template family is
\begin{equation}
  T_{{\rm moon},j}(\lambda) = I_\odot(\lambda)\;
  \alpha_{\rm ROLO}(\lambda,\phi_0)\; B^{(11)}_j(\lambda),
\end{equation}
for $j = 1,\dots,15$, where $I_\odot$ is the solar spectrum
\citep{Meftah2018}, $\alpha_{\rm ROLO}$ the disk-integrated lunar albedo
\citep{Kieffer2005} at a fiducial phase angle $\phi_0 = 30^\circ$, and
$B^{(11)}_j$ are cubic B-splines with 11 interior knots across the band.
Their weighted sum, the spline \emph{multiplier}, absorbs the wavelength
dependence of atmospheric scattering and the departure of the actual lunar
phase from $\phi_0$. The albedo carries the lunar mineral absorption bands,
which give the family a distinctive shape. At this knot spacing no adjacent
pair of basis functions is correlated above $|\rho| = 0.99$.

\subsection{Zodiacal light}
\label{sec:zodifam}

The zodiacal light is sunlight scattered by interplanetary dust, slightly
reddened. Its family is
\begin{equation}
  T_{{\rm zodi},j}(\lambda) = I_\odot(\lambda)\,
  \left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_0}\right)^{0.26} B^{(1)}_j(\lambda),
\end{equation}
for $j = 1,\dots,5$, with $\lambda_0 = 5000$\,\AA{} and a single interior
knot. A curvature penalty (Section~\ref{sec:qp}) further restricts the
multiplier, so it is nearly monotone. Moonlight and zodiacal light have to be
separate families. In a single family the moon templates absorb the smooth
zodiacal continuum on dark, low-ecliptic-latitude observations, and the moon
coefficients take on a mixed identity that no transfer can learn, since the
two emitters depend on disjoint geometries.

\subsection{Diffuse airglow continuum}
\label{sec:diffuse}

Three fixed templates, following the PALACE components
\citep{Noll2024,Noll2025}, describe the broadband airglow continuum. For
HO$_2$ and FeO (the ``orange arc'') we interpolate the PALACE templates onto
the LVM grid. The O$_2$ continuum we re-derived on LVM sky spectra: the
canonical template peaks at 3220\,\AA, outside the LVM band, so only its tail
is constrained, and over $4200$--$5900$\,\AA{} it runs about twice too high
and biases the blue residual. With the canonical HO$_2$/FeO pair, FeO peaks
at 5966\,\AA, where FeO(VIS) is expected, and the fit attributes the
580--610\,nm feature to FeO rather than HO$_2$.

PALACE is calibrated at Cerro Paranal and LVM observes from Las Campanas, so
the absolute PALACE continuum levels are only indicative. In dark time (Moon
below the horizon) the fitted diffuse block amounts to 7.8\% of the
500--720\,nm airglow, against $3.3\pm0.8\%$ in the PALACE reference. No
basis choice or constraint we considered removes this factor of 2.4: either
the continuum at Las Campanas differs, or the block contains emission that
is not airglow.

\subsection{Physical model of the moonlight and zodiacal-light brightness}
\label{sec:mzmodel}

The identifiability constraints (Section~\ref{sec:constraints}) and three of
the transfer's context features (Section~\ref{sec:ctx}) need an absolute,
geometry-driven prediction of the moonlight and zodiacal-light spectra at a
given pointing. For this we use a single-scattering model evaluated at the
exposure mid-time.

\paragraph{Moonlight.} The illuminating spectrum is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  F_{\rm moon}(\lambda) = {}&I_\odot(\lambda;\,v_{\rm moon})\;
  \alpha_{\rm ROLO}(\lambda,\phi)\\
  &\times\Big(\frac{R_{\rm moon}}{d_{\rm moon}}\Big)^{2}
  \Big(\frac{1\,{\rm au}}{d_{\odot,\rm moon}}\Big)^{2}.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where $I_\odot$ is Doppler-shifted by the velocity $v_{\rm moon}$ of the
reflected light, $\phi$ is the signed lunar phase angle, $R_{\rm moon}$ is
the lunar radius, and $d_{\rm moon}$ and $d_{\odot,\rm moon}$ are the
Earth--Moon and Sun--Moon distances. At a target of airmass $X_t$ and angular
distance $\theta$ from the Moon, the scattered brightness is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  S_{\rm moon}(\lambda) = {}&H(h_{\rm moon})\,F_{\rm moon}(\lambda)\\
  &\times X_t\,e^{-\tau X_t}\,\Phi\big(\tau\,[X_{\rm moon}-X_t]\big)\\
  &\times\Big[\tau_{\rm R}\,P_{\rm R}(\theta) +
  0.97\,\tau_{\rm A}\,P_{\rm HG}(\theta)\Big]\,E(\lambda).
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where $\tau = \tau_{\rm R}+\tau_{\rm A}$ is the sum of the Rayleigh optical
depth and the aerosol optical depth
$\tau_{\rm A} = 0.0336\,(\lambda/\lambda_0)^{-1.38}$;
$\Phi(\delta) = (1-e^{-\delta})/\delta$ is the two-airmass extinction factor;
$P_{\rm R}$ is the Rayleigh phase function with depolarisation factor 0.0148
and $P_{\rm HG}$ the Henyey--Greenstein phase function \citep{Henyey1941}
with $g = 0.8$; $E(\lambda) = [1+3.5\,X_t\,\tau_{\rm R}(\lambda)]/
[1+3.5\,X_t\,\tau_{\rm R}(\lambda_0)]$ approximates the multiple-scattering
enhancement of the blue; and $X_{\rm moon}$ is the airmass of the Moon.
Scattered moonlight persists with the Moon just below the horizon, which we
model with the taper $H = \exp[-8.22\,\tanh(\max(-h_{\rm moon},0)/5^\circ)]$,
which gives $-0.70$\,dex at $h_{\rm moon} = -1^\circ$, $-2.72$\,dex at
$-5^\circ$ and saturates near $-3.6$\,dex.

\paragraph{Zodiacal light.} The zodiacal brightness is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  S_{\rm zodi}(\lambda) = {}&B_{500}(\Delta\lambda_\odot,\beta)\;
  10^{\,f(\Delta\lambda_\odot,\,\beta)}\\
  &\times\frac{\tilde I_\odot(\lambda;\,v_{\rm z})}{I_\odot(\lambda_0)}
  \Big(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_0}\Big)^{0.26}
  e^{-X_t(\tau_{\rm R}+\tau_{\rm A})/2}.
\end{split}
  \label{eq:zodimodel}
\end{equation}
where $B_{500}$ is the tabulated zodiacal brightness at 500\,nm
\citep{Leinert1998} as a function of the helio-ecliptic longitude
$\Delta\lambda_\odot \equiv |\lambda_{\rm ecl}-\lambda_\odot|$ and the
ecliptic latitude $\beta$, both geocentric; $\tilde I_\odot$ is the solar
spectrum broadened by 30\,km\,s$^{-1}$ and Doppler-shifted by the dust
velocity $v_{\rm z}$; the exponent 0.26 is the zodiacal reddening; the
factor $1/2$ in the extinction reflects forward scattering into the beam of
an extended source; and $f$ is the empirical correction described below.
We multiply both spectra by the fiber solid angle and project them onto the
LVM grid with the LSF of the spectrum. The constraints use two numbers from
the model, the zodiacal integral
$Z_{\rm pred} = \int S_{\rm zodi}\,\dd\lambda$ and the moonlight fraction
\begin{equation}
  f_{\rm pred} = \frac{\int S_{\rm moon}\,\dd\lambda}
  {\int S_{\rm moon}\,\dd\lambda + \int S_{\rm zodi}\,\dd\lambda}.
\end{equation}
Both components pass through the same conversion, so $f_{\rm pred}$ does not
depend on throughput or flux calibration.

A calibrated version of the model exists, in which the Rayleigh, aerosol and
zodiacal terms carry fitted scale factors exponential in $\ln X_t$,
$\ln X_{\rm moon}$, phase and $\cos\theta$. Those factors were fitted to the
moon-plus-zodi \emph{sum} only, so the split between the two components that
they imply is not independently calibrated. The zodiacal scale even grows
with airmass, as $X^{1.28}$, where extinction should make it fall, which
looks like absorbed moonlight. For the constraints we therefore use the
physics-only model, with all fitted scale factors set to unity except the
horizon taper. The two versions differ by a factor of 1.5--1.7 in absolute
zodiacal light.

\paragraph{Empirical correction to the zodiacal profile.} In dark time the
data set the fitted zodiacal amplitude (Section~\ref{sec:constraints}).
Comparing it with Equation~(\ref{eq:zodimodel}) at $f=0$ shows that, for LVM,
the tabulated profile falls off too steeply with ecliptic latitude: it is too
bright on the ecliptic and too faint toward the poles, and so it overstates
the zodiacal contrast between neighbouring pointings. Regressing fitted on
modelled differences between the science and sky pointings gives slopes of
only 0.45 (near) and 0.43 (far). We correct the profile with a multiplicative
term,
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
  f = \sum_{(i,j)} c_{ij}\,P_i(u)\,P_j(v),\\
  u = \frac{2\,\Delta\lambda_\odot}{180^\circ}-1, \qquad
  v = 2\sin|\beta| - 1,
\end{gathered}
  \label{eq:zodicorr}
\end{equation}
where the $P_i$ are Legendre polynomials of degree $\le 2$, with the
coefficients of Table~\ref{tab:zodicorr}.

We fitted the correction on 14\,979 dark pointings, pooling all three arms,
and treated pointings at a bound of the zodiacal bracket as censored (a Tobit
likelihood; \citealt{Tobin1958}). The fit included a Galactic-latitude term
and a log-airmass term alongside the ecliptic shape, but we use only the
ecliptic part. The other two absorb light that is not zodiacal: Galactic

reduces the dark-time scatter of fitted against predicted zodiacal light

the absolute levels better but drops the near-arm slope to 0.37. Over the
observed pointings the correction ranges from $-0.13$ to $+0.26$\,dex
(median $+0.06$). It enters $Z_{\rm pred}$ and, through it, $f_{\rm pred}$,
so the two constraints that use them stay mutually consistent.

\subsection{Noise model and pixel weights}
\label{sec:noise}

The fit weights each pixel by the inverse of a photon-noise variance
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{var}(y_i) = \frac{y_i\,s(\lambda_i)}{t_{\rm exp}\,\Delta\lambda\,N_{\rm eff}},
\end{equation}
where $s(\lambda)$ is the absolute spectral sensitivity of the instrument
(flux per detected count), $t_{\rm exp}$ is the exposure time and
$N_{\rm eff}$ is the effective number of fibers of the median. A floor of 5\%
of the median of the spectrum keeps the variance from vanishing. We
normalise the weights to unit mean over the good pixels of each spectrum,
which preserves their relative size across wavelength and leaves the scale
of the objective, and so the meaning of the regularisation strengths,
unchanged. The red carries about three times the blue's weight: the sky is
brighter there, but the sensitivity is about seven times better, so the
variance is lower. Compared with uniform weights, the photon weights lower
the $\chi^2$ by 11--14\% over the full band in all three arms, and by 2--4\%
in the blue.

With $N_{\rm eff}=1$ the same variance is the photon noise of a single
fiber, and this is the yardstick we use for the sky prediction throughout
(Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}). The median spectra are cleaner than any one
fiber, but the sky model is subtracted from individual fibers, so a model
residual matters only in comparison with the single-fiber noise; a $\chi^2$
of 1 against it means the model error equals that noise. For a typical
observation the single-fiber noise in a 0.5\,\AA{} pixel is about 8\% of the
sky flux over the full band and 9\% in the blue.

Pixels near the emission lines of the science target (Balmer lines,
[N\,\textsc{ii}], [S\,\textsc{ii}], [O\,\textsc{iii}] and others) carry no
information about the sky, so we give them zero weight. We centre these
\emph{science-line windows} on the measured H$\alpha$ velocity of the field
and mask them in all three spectra of an observation, so that the three fits
use identical pixels. None of these lines is in the basis, and unmasked they
would end up mostly in the OH templates.

\subsection{Estimation}
\label{sec:estimation}

\subsubsection{Quadratic program}
\label{sec:qp}

For a given LSF we obtain the coefficients from the regularised, weighted,
non-negative least-squares problem
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  \vect{c}^\star = \arg\min_{\substack{\vect{c}\ge 0\\ \mathbf{G}\vect{c}\le\vect{h}}}\;
  \Big[\,&\tfrac12\big\lVert \mathbf{W}^{1/2}(\mathbf{A}\vect{c}-\vect{y})\big\rVert_2^2\\
  &+ \lambda_{\rm m}\big\lVert \mathbf{D}^{(2)}\vect{c}_{\rm moon}\big\rVert_2^2
  + \lambda_{\rm z}\big\lVert \mathbf{D}^{(2)}\vect{c}_{\rm zodi}\big\rVert_2^2\Big].
\end{split}
  \label{eq:qp}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{W}$ is the diagonal matrix of pixel weights
(Section~\ref{sec:noise}), $\mathbf{D}^{(2)}$ is the second-difference
operator on adjacent spline coefficients, with
$\lambda_{\rm m} = \lambda_{\rm z} = 0.1$ per unit of scaled flux, and
$\mathbf{G}\vect{c}\le\vect{h}$ collects the linear identifiability
constraints of Section~\ref{sec:constraints}. The problem is convex, and we
solve it with the interior-point conic solver Clarabel \citep{Goulart2024}.

The columns of $\mathbf{A}$ differ in scale by many orders of magnitude, so
we rescale the problem before the solve. We divide the data by
$d = \max(\mathrm{rms}(\mathbf{W}^{1/2}\vect{y}),1)$, each column $k$ by
$d\,s_k$ with $s_k = \lVert(\mathbf{W}^{1/2}\mathbf{A})_{:,k}\rVert_2$, and
the regularisation operators consistently,
$\mathbf{D}^{(2)}\to\mathbf{D}^{(2)}\,\mathrm{diag}(1/s)$, and transform the
solution back afterwards. Constraints without a right-hand side are unchanged
by the rescaling; those with one scale with the data.

\subsubsection{Alternating refinement of continuum, LSF and lines}

The LSF, the continuum and the line amplitudes constrain one another, and the
problem is not jointly convex in all three, so we refine them in turn. The
fit begins with a pre-fit of the O$_2$ $b$-band shape and a joint solve of
Equation~(\ref{eq:qp}), with every family rendered with the reference LSF of
Section~\ref{sec:lsf}. Refinement cycles follow, each in three stages. The
first refits the continuum block with the line model held fixed, under all
continuum constraints of Section~\ref{sec:constraints}; it down-weights
pixels within $\pm2$\,\AA{} of the lines that carry 99\% of the line flux by
$5\times10^{-4}$, and residual outliers by a Huber weight beyond $3\sigma$.
The second refits the LSF of Equation~(\ref{eq:lsf}) in the $r$ and $z$
channels to the data minus continuum, keeping the $b$ channel at its
reference, and re-renders the line templates and the blue continuum
templates with the new LSF. The third refits the continuum and the line
block on that LSF, so that the fit statistics of the cycle refer to it. We
stop when, at every wavelength, the widths containing 50\% and 90\% of the
kernel change by less than 1\% and its centroid by less than 0.015\,\AA, and
the $\chi^2$ changes by less than 1\%, or after 30 cycles. We keep a cycle
only if all its stages succeed; otherwise the fit returns the last kept
state, to which the final coefficients, uncertainties and component spectra
then refer.

\subsection{Identifiability constraints}
\label{sec:constraints}

The data term alone leaves several of the partitions the transfer depends on
undetermined. Both continuum families are built on the solar spectrum, so the
fit can move flux between moonlight and zodiacal light almost freely: it
constrains their \emph{sum} well but not the \emph{division}. We measure the
colour of a component by its log--log slope, the slope of $\log$ flux against
$\log\lambda$. On 200 sky spectra stratified in lunar phase, the
unconstrained fit put the two colours in the wrong order on 164 of 168
moon-up spectra, with a moonlight slope of $+0.21$ where the physics gives
$-3.7$ and a zodiacal slope of $-4.04$ where it gives $-0.3$. It gave the
moon family 45\% of the continuum with the Moon $37^\circ$ below the
horizon, and its ``zodiacal light'' correlated with lunar illumination at
$\rho = +0.95$ but with the tabulated $B_{500}$ at only $\rho = 0.09$. The
split within the diffuse block is not identifiable either.

We therefore add hard linear inequalities, in the same cone as
$\vect{c}\ge0$; unlike penalties, they never trade against the likelihood.
A constraint \emph{binds} when the solution lies exactly on its bound, and
the quantity it limits is then \emph{pinned}: set by the constraint rather
than measured.

\paragraph{(a) Adjacent-knot ratio bounds.} For each continuum spline family
$f\in\{\rm moon, zodi\}$ and each adjacent pair of coefficients,
\begin{equation}
  \beta\,c^{(f)}_k \;\le\; c^{(f)}_{k+1} \;\le\; \beta^{-1} c^{(f)}_k,
  \qquad \beta = 0.7 .
\end{equation}
This limits how fast a family's multiplier may change with wavelength, so
neither family can take on the colour of the other. With $K$ basis functions
the total permitted swing across the band is $\beta^{-(K-1)}$, so the value
of $\beta$ is tied to the 15 moon and 5 zodiacal basis functions. The bounds
also forbid a spline that falls to zero within the band and re-emerges, a
trick the unconstrained fit used to lower its residual by collapsing the
moon multiplier across $5100$--$6900$\,\AA. Together with non-negativity,
they make each family either entirely positive or identically zero.

\paragraph{(b) Moon-share bracket.} With the component integrals
$u = \int T_{\rm moon}\vect{c}_{\rm moon}\,\dd\lambda$ and
$v = \int T_{\rm zodi}\vect{c}_{\rm zodi}\,\dd\lambda$, both linear in
$\vect{c}$, the moonlight fraction is bracketed as
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
  f_{\rm lo}\le\frac{u}{u+v}\le f_{\rm hi}
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\\
  (1-f_{\rm hi})\,u - f_{\rm hi}\,v\le 0,\quad
  f_{\rm lo}\,v - (1-f_{\rm lo})\,u\le 0 .
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
We centre the bracket on $f_{\rm pred}$ (Section~\ref{sec:mzmodel}) with a
multiplicative tolerance $\kappa_f = 3$ and clip it to
$[\epsilon, 1-\epsilon]$ with $\epsilon = 0.02$. Constraining the fraction
rather than either amplitude keeps the constraint independent of the flux
calibration. The floor $\epsilon$ protects the two regimes where the
prediction is least reliable: the below-horizon taper, which is an
extrapolation, and thin crescents, where the lunar albedo model runs out of
phase coverage.

\paragraph{(c) Absolute zodiacal anchor.} The bracket in (b) is relative: it
can only divide whatever total the two families hold, and it cannot stop both
from growing with the Moon. An absolute anchor can:
\begin{equation}
  \kappa_z^{-1}\,C\,Z_{\rm pred} \;\le\; v \;\le\; \kappa_z\,C\,Z_{\rm pred},
\end{equation}
with $\kappa_z = 2$ and $C = 1.6$. The calibration $C$ recentres the model
on dark time, where the data set the zodiacal light; there the median of
$\log_{10}(v/C Z_{\rm pred})$ is $-0.06$. The width $\kappa_z$ matches the
dark-time scatter of that ratio, about 0.15\,dex, so a tighter bracket would
clip real variation. Because $Z_{\rm pred}$ enters with a non-zero right-hand
side, it has to be on the flux scale of the data being fitted.

The anchor binds from above on most moon-up observations, and rightly so.
When we release it ($\kappa_z = 100$), the fit only re-partitions a conserved
continuum (to 0.5\%), and the released zodiacal excess follows the fractional
lunar illumination FLI, not the ecliptic ($\rho = +0.73$ at fixed $B_{500}$):
it is moonlight leaking into the zodiacal family.

\paragraph{(d) Diffuse species-ratio bracket.} Without further constraints
the fitted FeO/HO$_2$ flux ratio spreads over 10\,dex, and the fit drives
HO$_2$ to exactly zero on 23\% of observations. HO$_2$ emits only 5\% of its
flux below 9800\,\AA{} and is a featureless red continuum within the band.
The spread is not information: the three arms of one exposure, which look at
the same airglow a few degrees apart, disagree about the ratio by 0.63\,dex
at the median. We therefore bracket both species ratios about the sample's
geometric-median flux shares, 0.040, 0.703 and 0.258 for HO$_2$, FeO and
O$_2$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
  \Big|\log_{10}\frac{A_{\rm FeO}}{A_{\rm HO_2}} - \log_{10}\frac{0.703}{0.040}\Big| \le 0.2,\\
  \Big|\log_{10}\frac{A_{\rm O_2}}{A_{\rm HO_2}} - \log_{10}\frac{0.258}{0.040}\Big| \le 0.2 .
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
Each side of a ratio bound is one linear inequality, $A_k - R\,A_0 \le 0$.
Centring on the PALACE reference shares instead would cost 26\% of the blue
$\chi^2$, against 0.7\% for the sample median, which puts a number on the
site difference of Section~\ref{sec:diffuse}. The width costs almost
nothing, because the scatter it removes is degeneracy. On 500 stratified
observations the 5--95\% span of the ratio collapses from 10.3 to 0.40\,dex,
and near-zero HO$_2$ falls from 23\% to 0.2\% of observations, for $+2.5\%$
in blue $\chi^2$ and no measurable full-band cost. The constraint moves the
split between the diffuse and zodiacal families but hardly their sum: the
change in $\log_{10}$ of zodi plus diffuse stays below 0.012 at the 90th
percentile.

\paragraph{(e) Moon-gated cap on the diffuse block.} The diffuse species are
mesospheric chemiluminescence and cannot depend on the Moon. Yet the ratio
$A_{\rm diff}/A_{\rm OH}$ rises with the predicted moonlight fraction at
$\rho = +0.72$, while airmass and OH brightness give $|\rho| < 0.1$; at full
Moon about three quarters of the fitted FeO is moonlight. We normalise by OH
because it is the best-determined airglow amplitude and is itself insensitive
to the Moon, which makes the leak measurable. The cap is
\begin{equation}
  A_{\rm HO_2} + A_{\rm FeO} + A_{\rm O_2} \;\le\; 10^{\,c_0 + W} A_{\rm OH},
\end{equation}
with $c_0 = -0.649$, the dark-time median of the ratio, and
$W = 0.15$\,dex. The line model is held fixed in the continuum step, so
$A_{\rm OH}$ is a constant there and the cap is one inequality with a
right-hand side, which we re-install in every cycle. The cap is one-sided,
because the leak has one direction. We apply it only where
$f_{\rm pred} > 0.6$, because the dark-time scatter of the ratio
(0.22\,dex) is real and an ungated cap costs 15--32\% of the blue $\chi^2$.
It caps the whole block rather than FeO alone; a cap on FeO alone merely
made the ratio bracket (d) bind instead.

On 1000 random observations the cap binds on 70\% of the gated ones and
leaves none above it. There it reduces FeO by 54\% and the block by 42\%,
sends 98.6\% of the released flux to the moon family and none to the
zodiacal family (which the anchor protects), and conserves the continuum
total to 0.03\%. It costs 1.3\% of the blue $\chi^2$ where it binds and
nothing measurable elsewhere, and it removes 77\% of the moon-attributable
excess of the 580--610\,nm feature. The remaining correlation is
structural: in the faintest quartile of gated moonlight the ratio already
lies below the cap, so no flat ceiling can remove it.

They also have a symmetric consequence to keep in mind whenever one reads a

\subsection{Retry on reversal}
\label{sec:retry}

We call a spectrum \emph{reversed} when its fitted moonlight is redder than
its fitted zodiacal light, $s_{\rm moon} > s_{\rm zodi}$, where $s$ is the
log--log slope of the reconstructed component. We test only where both
families carry flux, $0.05\le u/(u+v)\le0.95$. A reversed spectrum is
mislabelled rather than noisy: its moon coefficients describe zodiacal
light. After constraints (a)--(c), reversals are near-ties (all within 2.0
of the boundary in the separation metric) and occur on 1--5\% of testable
spectra. They are more common at low lunar illumination and altitude, and
3.6 times more common in the science arm than in the far arm, consistent
with faint-source continuum in the science fibers. Their amplitudes are
nearly right, because the data barely distinguish the two branches
($\rho({\rm moon},{\rm zodi}) = -0.95$). A refit with a tighter ratio bound,
$\beta = 0.85$, recovered all 18 reversed spectra of a test set, for a
median $\chi^2$ increase of 2.8\% and a change in the moonlight fraction only
in the fourth decimal.

The decomposition therefore retries every reversed spectrum at
$\beta = 0.85$ and keeps the retry if it is no longer reversed, recording
the retry and its outcome in the flags (Section~\ref{sec:outputs}). The
photon weighting of Section~\ref{sec:noise} shifts the partition enough to
raise the first-fit reversal count, and the retry absorbs the increase.

\subsection{Coefficient uncertainties}
\label{sec:uncertainties}

At the optimum the coefficients split into an active set $\mathcal{A}$
($c_k > 0$) and coefficients pinned at zero. On the active set the
regularised Fisher matrix is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  \mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{A}} = {}&\mathbf{A}_{:,\mathcal{A}}^{\!\top}\mathbf{W}
  \mathbf{A}_{:,\mathcal{A}}
  + 2\lambda_{\rm m}\,\mathbf{D}^{(2)\top}\mathbf{D}^{(2)}\big|_{\mathcal{A},\rm moon}\\
  &+ 2\lambda_{\rm z}\,\mathbf{D}^{(2)\top}\mathbf{D}^{(2)}\big|_{\mathcal{A},\rm zodi},
\end{split}
\end{equation}
and the covariance is $\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}$, which we inflate by
$\max(\chi^2_\nu,1)$ to absorb model misfit. Pinned coefficients have no
defined uncertainty. The marginal errors
$\sigma_k = [\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}]_{kk}^{1/2}$ misrepresent
near-collinear blocks: adjacent moon-spline coefficients remain correlated at
$|\rho|\approx0.88$, so $\sigma_k$ overstates the uncertainty of each knot
and understates that of joint quantities such as the block amplitude. We
therefore also keep the full covariance blocks
$\mathbf{\Sigma}_g = [\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}]_{\mathcal{A}\cap g,\,\mathcal{A}\cap g}$
for the moon and zodiacal families.

\subsection{Selection of the training sample}
\label{sec:selection}

We train the transfer only on observations whose coefficients mean what their
names say, and reject an observation if any of its three spectra fails one
of the following tests:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Field exclusion}: pointings within $10^\circ$ of the Large and
        Small Magellanic Clouds, where bright stellar populations and ionised
        gas at velocities close to the airglow blend with the science-arm
        fit.
  \item \emph{Physical context}: the pointing is above the horizon, the
        airmass is at most 3, and the van Rhijn factors are finite and
        $\ge1$.
  \item \emph{Fit quality}: reduced $\chi^2$ below the 90th percentile of
        the sample.
  \item \emph{Reversal}: moon/zodiacal reversal after the retry.
  \item \emph{Coefficient runaway}: hard limits on FeO and K\,\textsc{i},
        and a robust limit on the mean OH coefficient, which catches
        spectra whose OH block ran away by many orders of magnitude.
  \item \emph{Diffuse collapse}: all three diffuse species below $10^{-3}$
        of their median. The fit put no diffuse continuum at all and let the
        zodiacal family carry it. The distribution is bimodal, with nothing
        between $10^{-6}$ and $10^{-2}$ of the median.
  \item \emph{Science-continuum colour excess}: the red/blue continuum colour
        of the science spectrum exceeds that of the sky arms by more than
        0.05. The fiber carries continuum that the sky model cannot
        represent, and the moon family has absorbed it.
  \item \emph{Outlier clipping}: iterative $\kappa\sigma$ clipping
        ($\kappa = 8$) of the concatenated three-arm coefficient vector, and
        of the OH block with $\kappa = 6$ on a robust scale.
\end{enumerate}
These tests select the training sample only. In production we predict every
observation, and the flags of Table~\ref{tab:flags} report what the tests
would have concluded.

\section{Geometric normalisation and effective extinction}
\label{sec:geometry}

The three arms of an observation are simultaneous but have different zenith
angles, so each sees a different slant path through the emitting layers and
the atmosphere. This part of the difference between the arms is known
physics, and we remove it in closed form before the transfer, so that the
network does not have to learn it from limited data.

\subsection{Scaling law}

For a thin emitting shell at height $h$, the observed amplitude at zenith
angle $z$ and airmass $X$ relates to the zenith-equivalent amplitude by
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
  A_{\rm obs} = A_{\rm int}\;V(z;h)\;10^{-0.4\,k(\lambda)\,[X(z)-1]},\\
  V(z;h) = \left[1-\Big(\frac{R_\oplus}{R_\oplus+h}\Big)^2\sin^2 z\right]^{-1/2},
\end{gathered}
  \label{eq:vanrhijn}
\end{equation}
where $V$ is the van Rhijn factor \citep{vanRhijn1921} and $k$ is the
extinction coefficient. Because the observer sits inside a curved shell, $V$
saturates toward the horizon, at 6.0 for $h = 87$\,km and 3.4 for
$h = 285$\,km. A plane-parallel $\sec z$ would overstate the enhancement by
4\% at $z = 60^\circ$ for a mesospheric layer and by 12\% for an ionospheric
one, and since that error is a smooth function of $z$, it would not average
out.

\subsection{Normalisation and its inverse}
\label{sec:normalisation}

For every airglow coefficient of each of the three spectra,
\begin{equation}
  c^{\rm int}_{r,k} = \frac{c^{\rm obs}_{r,k}}{V(z_r;h_{g(k)})\,10^{-0.4\,k_k\,(X_r-1)}},
\end{equation}
where $g(k)$ is the group of coefficient $k$ and $k_k$ its effective
extinction coefficient. The transfer learns and predicts these
zenith-equivalent amplitudes, and we multiply the prediction by the
science-pointing factor to restore observed amplitudes. The order matters:
we normalise the physical coefficients \emph{before} the non-linear
transforms and the robust scaling of Section~\ref{sec:compression}, neither
of which commutes with a division by $V$. For example,
$(\sqrt{c}-m)/V \neq \sqrt{c/V}-m$, so a correction applied after the
transforms would be wrong at the order of $V$ itself.

Because the reference is $X = 1$ rather than $X = 0$, the normalised
amplitude is zenith-equivalent: it still carries one airmass of extinction,
which cancels in the transfer, and it is not a physical layer emissivity.

\subsection{Per-coefficient effective wavelength}

Extinction varies strongly across the LVM range; even within the OH Meinel
system $k(\lambda)$ changes by nearly a factor of two, so one wavelength per
group will not do. We assign each coefficient $j$ the intensity-weighted
centroid of its own template,
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_{\rm eff}(j) = \frac{\sum_i \lambda_i\,|T_j(\lambda_i)|}{\sum_i |T_j(\lambda_i)|},
\end{equation}
computed from the design matrix of the decomposition itself, so that the
rotational and vibrational populations, the LSF and the band blending are
included by construction. Named atomic lines with no support on the grid
fall back to their laboratory wavelengths.

\subsection{Effective extinction}
\label{sec:extinction}

Three facts determine how to choose $k$.

\paragraph{Only differences matter.} The sky-to-science transfer of an
airglow amplitude is
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
  R = \frac{V(z_{\rm sci})}{V(z_{\rm sky})}\,10^{-0.4\,k\,(X_{\rm sci}-X_{\rm sky})},\\
  \frac{\partial\ln R}{\partial k} = -0.4\ln10\,\Delta X \approx -0.92\,\Delta X .
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
The absolute value of $k$ cancels. A per-night error $\delta k$ enters only
as $0.92\,\delta k\,\Delta X$, which is 0.2--0.7\% for typical aerosol
variability ($\delta k\sim0.02$--$0.04$\,mag per airmass) and
$\Delta X\sim0.2$, an order of magnitude below the gravity-wave floor. We
therefore do not model per-exposure extinction.

\paragraph{A stellar extinction curve is wrong for an extended source.}
A point source loses its scattered photons, but for a source that fills the
sky they are largely replaced by photons scattered in from neighbouring
lines of sight. Scattering dominates $k$ wherever airglow matters, so the
stellar curve over-corrects, most severely in the blue, and the error is a
coherent bias rather than random scatter.

\paragraph{Height and extinction are degenerate.} For $z\le60^\circ$,
$\ln V(z;h)$ and $X-1$ are collinear to $\rho>0.995$. The data constrain only
the product $V\,10^{-0.4k(X-1)}$, which is all the normalisation needs.

\paragraph{Estimator.} We therefore fit an effective $k_{\rm eff}$, using the
airglow as its own source. For coefficient $j$ in the simultaneous near and
far spectra,
\begin{equation}
  \ln\frac{A_{\rm nr}}{A_{\rm fr}} - \ln\frac{V_{\rm nr}}{V_{\rm fr}}
  = -0.4\ln10\;k_{\rm eff}\,(X_{\rm nr}-X_{\rm fr}) + \varepsilon ,
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon$ is the gravity-wave fluctuation between the two lines of
sight, which has zero mean in the logarithm and is uncorrelated with the
airmass difference. The intrinsic emissivity cancels, because both arms
measure the same coefficient at the same instant. The fitted $k_{\rm eff}$
absorbs the multiple-scattering correction, the site aerosol level and any
error in the assumed layer height; given the degeneracy above, we fix the
height and fit only $k_{\rm eff}$. We fit all observations at once rather
than apply an amplitude threshold, which would select on the outcome and
correlate the residual with the sign of $\Delta X$. The errors are clustered
by observation, because all coefficients of an observation share one
fluctuation. We monitor split-half values and the intercept, which should
vanish; a significant intercept would indicate a throughput offset between
the sky channels. We interpolate the fitted values in wavelength over
well-constrained bins, use the generic site curve elsewhere, and clip the
result to $[0, k_{\rm stellar}]$, since multiple scattering makes the
stellar curve an upper bound.

\subsection{Moonlight and zodiacal light are exempt}
\label{sec:exempt}

Neither moonlight nor zodiacal light is thin-shell emission, so both keep
unit geometry factors. Scattering geometry and ecliptic geometry,
respectively, govern their transfer between pointings, and the network
receives both as context (Section~\ref{sec:ctx}). For both, the airmass
difference affects the extinction only at the percent level.

\section{Learned sky-to-science transfer}
\label{sec:ml}

\subsection{Formulation}

Given the coefficients of the near and far arms of an observation, and the
observing \emph{context} of all three arms (features describing the
pointing, lunar, solar and ecliptic geometry, time and solar activity;
Section~\ref{sec:ctx}), the transfer predicts the science-arm coefficients
$\hat{\vect c}_{\rm sci}$, from which we reconstruct the predicted sky
$\hat y_{\rm sci}(\lambda) = \mathbf{A}^{\rm obs}_{\rm sci}\,\hat{\vect c}_{\rm sci}$
with the science spectrum's own LSF and transmission. The transfer works in
the zenith-equivalent frame of Section~\ref{sec:geometry}, and its target,
the \emph{truth}, is the decomposition of the science spectrum itself.

The coefficients span many decades, so the network does not see them
directly. An invertible transform maps each to a \emph{score}, a number of
order unity (Section~\ref{sec:compression}). We say an error measured on the
scores is in \emph{score space}, where every coefficient counts equally
however little flux it carries, and an error measured on the reconstructed
spectra is in \emph{flux space}, where each coefficient counts in proportion
to the flux it contributes. Flux space is what matters for sky subtraction.

\subsection{Coefficient groups}

We divide the 388 coefficients into six \emph{groups} whose members share a
physical driver (Table~\ref{tab:groups}). The groups follow the emitter
families, except that we split the atomic lines into mesopause (atomic) and
thermospheric (ionospheric) lines and put the O$_2$ band with OH in the
mesospheric group. Each group has its own output \emph{head} (the final
network layers that predict its scores), its own blend weight
(Section~\ref{sec:blend}) and its own loss term. Moonlight and zodiacal
light form separate groups because they depend on disjoint context, lunar
and ecliptic geometry respectively.

\subsection{Per-group transforms}
\label{sec:compression}

An invertible transform, fitted on the training observations, maps each group
block $\vect c_g$ to a score vector $\vect s_g$. We first divide the
coefficients by a robust scale $\vect\rho_g$, the median of the positive
training values in the near arm, and then apply an elementwise transform
$f_g$: $\operatorname{asinh}$ for the mesospheric group, whose coefficients
span many decades and include lines near zero, and $\sqrt{\cdot}$ for the
other groups. We standardise the result $\vect u_g$ and, for the moon group,
rotate it onto its principal components,
$\vect s_g = \big((\vect u_g-\vect\mu_g)/\vect\sigma_g\big)\,\mathbf{B}_g$
(for the other groups $\mathbf{B}_g$ is the identity). We keep all 15 moon
components, so the rotation decorrelates the spline coefficients without
reducing their number. Finally we robust-scale the scores of all three arms
by their training median and interquartile range. The inverse transform
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\vect c}_g = f_g^{-1}\!\big(\hat{\vect s}_g\,\mathbf{B}_g^{\top}\odot\vect\sigma_g + \vect\mu_g\big)\odot\vect\rho_g
\end{equation}
is clipped to the per-coefficient training range, so that $\sinh$ cannot
blow up an outlying prediction.

\subsection{Observing context}
\label{sec:ctx}

We describe each pointing by 42 context features (Table~\ref{tab:ctx}),
computed for the science pointing and for both sky pointings. Positions of
the Sun and Moon are topocentric at Las Campanas at the exposure mid-time,
and the helio-ecliptic longitude uses the geocentric Sun. Three features come
from the physical model of Section~\ref{sec:mzmodel}: the predicted ratio of
moonlight at the science pointing to that at the near pointing (zero with
the Moon down), $\log_{10}Z_{\rm pred}$ and $f_{\rm pred}$. The last two
define the zodiacal anchor and the moon-share bracket, so a head needs them
to reproduce a pinned amplitude.

Figure~\ref{fig:context-amplitude} shows how the fitted amplitudes vary with
twelve of these features, and why we give the heads different context. The
moonlight is bimodal: it sits at its floor with the Moon down and rises by
three decades with the Moon up, closely following lunar altitude, phase and
illumination. The zodiacal light stays within about 0.3\,dex and varies
mainly with solar elongation and ecliptic latitude. OH and the atomic lines
brighten toward low altitude and high airmass, as the van Rhijn factor
predicts, and OH fades during the first half of the night. The ionospheric
lines fade by about an order of magnitude through the night as the F region
recombines after dusk. The diffuse species vary only weakly, mostly with
altitude, although the O$_2$ continuum still rises slightly with lunar
phase, a remnant of the moonlight leak that constraint (e) limits.

\subsection{Architecture}
\label{sec:arch}

The network (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}) follows three principles. One encoder,
with shared weights, encodes both sky arms, so an arm's encoding does not
depend on which telescope produced it. Each group has its own head. And the
zodiacal and diffuse-continuum groups bypass the shared layers (the
\emph{trunk}) through isolated branches that see their physical drivers
directly. Below, $\vect s^{\rm nr}, \vect s^{\rm fr}\in\mathbb{R}^{388}$ are the
scores of the two sky arms and $\vect x^{\rm nr}, \vect x^{\rm fr},
\vect x^{\rm sci}\in\mathbb{R}^{42}$ the scaled context vectors. All hidden
layers use layer normalisation \citep{Ba2016} and GELU activations
\citep{Hendrycks2016}.

\paragraph{Arm encoder.} The same network encodes each sky arm,
\begin{equation}
  \vect e^{\rm nr} = \mathrm{MLP}_{\rm sky}\big([\vect s^{\rm nr};\vect x^{\rm nr}]\big),
  \qquad
  \vect e^{\rm fr} = \mathrm{MLP}_{\rm sky}\big([\vect s^{\rm fr};\vect x^{\rm fr}]\big),
\end{equation}
with widths $430\to768\to384$.

\paragraph{Context encoder.} A separate network embeds the science context,
$\vect e^{\rm ctx} = \mathrm{MLP}_{\rm ctx}(\vect x^{\rm sci})$, with width
96.

\paragraph{Symmetric fusion.} We combine the arm encodings as
\begin{equation}
  \vect z = \big[\tfrac12(\vect e^{\rm nr}+\vect e^{\rm fr});\;
  \vect e^{\rm nr}-\vect e^{\rm fr};\;
  |\vect e^{\rm nr}-\vect e^{\rm fr}|;\;
  \vect e^{\rm ctx}\big]\in\mathbb{R}^{1248}.
\end{equation}
The signed difference gives the direction of an asymmetry between the arms
(for example, which arm is closer to the Moon), and its absolute value a
magnitude the trunk can use regardless of orientation. The mean and the
absolute difference are symmetric under exchange of the arms; the signed
difference, like the blend of Section~\ref{sec:blend}, deliberately is not,
because the near/far labels carry physical meaning, the angular distance to
the science field. The east/west identity of the telescopes enters only as a
context feature.

\paragraph{Trunk and heads.} A trunk
$\vect h = \mathrm{MLP}_{\rm trunk}(\vect z)$ with widths $320\to160$ feeds
two-layer heads ($160\to192\to n_g$) for the moon, mesospheric, ionospheric
and atomic groups. The heads are linear at the output and predict scores.

\paragraph{Isolated zodiacal branch.} The zodiacal head bypasses the trunk.
It reads the near- and far-arm zodiacal scores and a restricted context set
of 19 features from all three pointings: the zodiacal-geometry features, the
moon-geometry features and the two anchor inputs (Table~\ref{tab:routing}),
$19\times3+2\times5 = 67$ inputs in all. They pass through a branch of
widths $64\to32$ and a linear output of 5 scores. Moon information dominates
the trunk's 160-dimensional bottleneck, so bypassing it keeps moon-driven
and activity-driven variation out of the zodiacal prediction and gives the
head direct access to the ecliptic geometry. We include the moon features
because, with the Moon down, the zodiacal family also carries residual
continuum whose transfer depends on the lunar regime.

\paragraph{Isolated continuum branch.} The diffuse-continuum head likewise
bypasses the trunk. It reads 9 moon-scattering features and the two arms'
continuum scores ($9\times3 + 2\times3 = 33$ inputs), through widths
$128\to64$ and a head $64\to64\to3$. We added it because the continuum
residuals depend non-linearly on moon geometry in a way the shared trunk did
not capture.

\paragraph{Additive moon--zodiacal coupling.} Moonlight and zodiacal light
both depend on the joint state of lunar scattering and ecliptic geometry. To
let the two heads share that information without breaking the isolation of
the zodiacal branch, a coupling branch reads the union of their context
features (20 features from the three arms) and the moon and zodiacal scores
of both sky arms, $20\times3 + 2\times(15+5) = 100$ inputs, through widths
$64\to32$. Linear projections $P_{\rm moon}\in\mathbb{R}^{15\times32}$ and
$P_{\rm zodi}\in\mathbb{R}^{5\times32}$ add the resulting latent $\vect\ell$
to both outputs. We initialise the projections to zero, so at the start of
training the coupled network computes exactly the uncoupled one. The four
other groups receive no coupling term.

\paragraph{Output.} For every group the predicted score is an arm blend plus
the head residual (Section~\ref{sec:blend}):
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  \hat{\vect s}_g = {}&\alpha_g\,\vect s^{\rm nr}_g + (1-\alpha_g)\,\vect s^{\rm fr}_g\\
  &+ \hat{\vect s}^{\,\rm head}_g + \Delta_g,
\end{split}
  \label{eq:output}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta_g = P_g\,\vect\ell$ for the moon and zodiacal groups and
$\Delta_g = 0$ for the others.

\subsection{Arm-blend prior}
\label{sec:blend}

A good first estimate of the science coefficients is an interpolation between
the two sky arms. The blend term of Equation~(\ref{eq:output}) makes that
estimate explicit, and the heads predict a correction on top of it. For the
mesospheric, ionospheric and atomic groups, whose transfer is isotropic on
the LVM baseline, the blend weight $\alpha_g$ is a learned scalar clipped to
$[10^{-3}, 1-10^{-3}]$. For the moon, zodiacal and continuum groups it
depends on the science context,
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_g(\vect x^{\rm sci}) = \sigma\big(\vect w_g\cdot\vect x^{\rm sci}_\alpha + b_g\big),
\end{equation}
where $\vect x^{\rm sci}_\alpha$ contains the horizon step, the ecliptic
latitude and the airmass. We start $\vect w_g$ at zero and $b_g$ at the logit
of the initial blend, so training begins from a context-independent blend.
(For the ionospheric group a context-dependent blend does worse.) The blend
reads only the science context, so it can learn how the best mixture depends
on conditions, but not which arm is better placed.

The blend weight is hard to learn. A head can absorb the systematic part of
a mis-set blend, but not the arm noise the blend lets through, so a wrong
$\alpha$ costs accuracy while producing little gradient, and the sigmoid
weakens that gradient further. We therefore train the blend parameters with
a learning rate 30 times that of the rest of the network; $\alpha$ then
reaches the same value whichever side of the optimum it starts from.

Figure~\ref{fig:blend-alpha} shows how the blend weights evolve during
training. All six favour the near arm. The ionospheric, moon and atomic
weights settle within a few tens of epochs at 0.90--0.96; the continuum
weight settles near 0.87, and the zodiacal weight climbs slowly to the same
value. The mesospheric weight keeps falling for most of the training and
ends near 0.55, close to an equal mix of the two arms. Copying the near arm
is also a better naive predictor than the arm mean for all six groups. A
zodiacal weight near 0.9, rather than the 0.5 one would expect of a smooth,
isotropic emitter, fits the picture of a zodiacal family that also carries
continuum that is not zodiacal light.

\subsection{Loss}
\label{sec:loss}

The loss mixes flux space and score space. We compare the moon, zodiacal and
continuum groups, whose errors matter in proportion to their brightness, as
reconstructed spectra, and the line groups in score space, where the robust
scaling makes residuals comparable across coefficients of very different
amplitude.

\paragraph{Flux-space term.} For $g\in\{\text{moon, zodi, continuum}\}$ we
convert the predicted and target scores back to coefficients with a
differentiable version of the inverse transform of
Section~\ref{sec:compression}, multiply them by the science-arm geometry
factor, and project them onto the group's templates $\mathbf{T}_g$ on the
full native wavelength grid. For observation $r$ the term is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}^{\rm flux}_{g,r} = s_g\,\frac{1}{n_\lambda}\sum_{\lambda}
  w_{r\lambda}\,\big[(\hat{\vect c}_{g,r}-\vect c_{g,r})^{\!\top}\mathbf{T}_g\big]_\lambda^2 ,
\end{equation}
where $w_{r\lambda} = 1/(y_{r\lambda}\,s(\lambda))$ is the photon-noise
weight of the observed science flux (Section~\ref{sec:noise}), with a
variance floor of 5\% of the median and normalised to unit mean for each
observation, and $s_g$ scales the median flux term to the median
score-space term, so that the group weights below keep their meaning.
Unlike a score-space loss, this term weights each observation by the
brightness of its target: a given score error under a bright Moon, where the
sky subtraction is most at risk, costs up to $10^5$ times more than in dark
time, in proportion to its effect on the delivered spectrum. Each group
enters through its own templates only.

\paragraph{Log-amplitude term.} The flux term is absolute, so the brightest
observations dominate it. For the moon group we add a scale-free companion
that penalises fractional amplitude errors equally at all brightnesses:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}^{\rm amp}_{{\rm moon},r} = \lambda_{\rm amp}\left[\log\frac{\sum_\lambda\hat f_r(\lambda)+\epsilon}{\sum_\lambda f_r(\lambda)+\epsilon}\right]^2,
  \qquad \lambda_{\rm amp} = 5,
\end{equation}
where $f$ is the moon flux and $\epsilon$ is 5\% of its median training
amplitude, so that very faint moonlight does not dominate the ratio. The
zodiacal group gets no such term: with its amplitude pinned on most moon-up
observations, a penalty would only trade spectral shape for an amplitude the
network already reproduces.

\paragraph{Score-space term.} For the mesospheric, ionospheric and atomic
groups,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}^{\rm score}_{g,r} = \frac{1}{n_g}\sum_{k=1}^{n_g} w^{\rm pe}_{g,k,r}\;
  \mathrm{Huber}\big(\hat s_{g,k,r}-s_{g,k,r}\big),
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{Huber}$ is quadratic for residuals below one (in scaled units)
and linear beyond. The per-element weights $w^{\rm pe} = 1/\sigma_s^2$
propagate the decomposition uncertainties (Section~\ref{sec:uncertainties})
through the transform to first order,
$\sigma_{s,k} = \lVert\nabla_{\vect c_g} s_k\rVert\,\sigma_{\vect c_g}$,
divided by the robust score scale. We floor the score uncertainty $\sigma_s$
at 5\% of its median for each coefficient (20\% for the mesospheric and
atomic groups, whose uncertainties have tails spanning three to six decades)
and normalise the weights to unit mean over the training observations.

\paragraph{Total.} The total loss is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{6}\sum_g \frac{m_g}{\sqrt{n_g}}\;
  \Big\langle \mathcal{L}_{g,r}\Big\rangle_r ,
\end{equation}
with group weights $m_{\rm moon} = 3$, $m_{\rm zodi} = 2$ and $m_g = 1$
otherwise, and $\mathcal{L}_{g,r}$ the flux (plus amplitude) term or the
score term as appropriate. All observations carry equal weight.

\subsection{Constraint-derived amplitudes}
\label{sec:rules}

Where a constraint pins one of the continuum amplitudes
(Section~\ref{sec:constraints}), the target is a known function of geometry,
so we derive it at prediction time instead of learning it. Both rules rescale
a whole coefficient block and leave the predicted spectral shape unchanged.
Both are idempotent and linear in the coefficients, so the ensemble mean of
members that satisfy them satisfies them too. We apply the zodiacal rule
first, because the moonlight rule reads the predicted zodiacal amplitude.

\paragraph{Bright Moon: the zodiacal ceiling.} Where the anchor binds, the
zodiacal amplitude equals $\kappa_z C Z_{\rm pred}$ exactly. With a
constant $S$ fitted on the training observations, the rule clips the
predicted zodiacal amplitude to $S Z_{\rm pred}$ everywhere. This is a hard
upper bound on the fitted value, exceeded by 0.01\% of observations and
never by more than 0.002\,dex, so clipping can only move a prediction toward
the truth. Where $f_{\rm pred} > 0.6$, the rule also sets the amplitude to
the ceiling if the network already predicts at least 90\% of it. This second
condition leaves alone the observations that really are below the ceiling,
which the network places well below it. The loss stays as it is: the network
learns the ceiling, and its own prediction separates pinned from unpinned
observations.

\paragraph{Dark time: the moonlight floor.} Where the moon-share bracket has
collapsed onto its floor, the moonlight is not measured. In training we
therefore make the flux loss blind to the amplitude on these observations:
we rescale the prediction to the true amplitude before the flux term (keeping
the scale factor in the gradient, so that the amplitude gradient vanishes
identically) and drop the log-amplitude term, so only the shape is trained.
At prediction we restore the amplitude from the near arm's measured
moonlight-to-zodiacal ratio,
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  \textstyle\int{\rm moon}_{\rm sci} = {}&\mathrm{clip}\big(
  (\textstyle\int{\rm moon}/\!\int{\rm zodi})_{\rm nr},\,0,\,R\big)\\
  &\times\textstyle\int{\rm zodi}_{\rm sci},
\end{split}
\end{equation}
with the fitted constant $R\approx\epsilon/(1-\epsilon)$ as a fallback when
the near arm is unusable. We transfer the ratio rather than set it to $R$,
because the dark-time moon share is bimodal: 96\% of these observations sit
on the floor and about 3\% want no moonlight at all.

We select the regime by $f_{\rm pred}\le\epsilon/\kappa_f$, not by lunar
altitude. Scattered moonlight with the Moon a few degrees below the horizon
is real, and the horizon taper multiplies phase and separation: a thin
crescent $2^\circ$ down contributes nothing, while a full Moon $5^\circ$ down
still does. No fixed altitude cut matches this gate in both coverage and
exactness (Table~\ref{tab:moongate}).

\paragraph{Degenerate continuum.} The dark-time observations that want no
moonlight are failed fits rather than a physical state: their median
$\chi^2$ is 15 times that of other dark-time observations, and all of their
continuum is in the zodiacal family. We flag them at prediction time from
the two sky arms alone, when both have a moonlight-to-zodiacal ratio below
$R/2$. The flag has 88\% precision at 95\% recall, affects 1.9\% of
observations, and never fires with the Moon up.

\subsection{Bias calibration}
\label{sec:calibration}

After training we fit a multiplicative bias correction on the training and
validation observations and apply it at prediction time. For the moon and
zodiacal groups it is one factor per coefficient,
$\ell_k = \bar c^{\rm true}_k/\bar c^{\rm pred}_k$, clipped to $[0.7,1.4]$,
because a single factor cannot remove the spectral tilt of the bias. We fit
the moon correction only where the network, not the dark-time rule, predicts
the amplitude, and the zodiacal correction separately in three
lunar-altitude regimes, with smooth transitions between them. For the other
groups we fit one factor per group on the amplitude
(Equation~\ref{eq:amplitude}), clipped to $[0.5, 2]$. The amplitude, not the
mean coefficient, is the right quantity here: for a group whose templates
carry very different flux the two differ and can even disagree in sign, and
across the 357 OH templates the integrals span nine decades and are
anti-correlated with coefficient size.

\subsection{Training}
\label{sec:training}

We group the observations by night and assign the nights to training,
validation and test sets, stratified by lunar phase. Keeping whole nights
together matters because the sky of neighbouring exposures within a night is
correlated, and a random split would leak. We call the validation and test
observations together the \emph{held-out} observations; none of them is used
to fit network weights.

We train each network with AdamW \citep{Loshchilov2019}, with learning rate
$10^{-3}$, weight decay $2\times10^{-4}$, gradient clipping at 1 and batches
of 512, for up to 400 epochs with early stopping on the validation loss and
a patience of 100 epochs. The blend parameters get no weight decay and the
raised learning rate of Section~\ref{sec:blend}. The prediction is the mean
of an ensemble of ten networks trained with different random seeds on the
same split, and we report the ensemble spread as an uncertainty.

Figure~\ref{fig:training-loss} shows the training and validation loss of
each member. The training loss falls by more than an order of magnitude and
is still falling slowly at 400 epochs. The validation loss flattens after
about 100 epochs at roughly twenty times the training loss, and reaches its
minimum anywhere between about 50 and 370 epochs depending on the member;
five of the ten members stopped early. The brief spikes shared by both
curves are single unstable epochs, from which training recovers.

\subsection{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

The deliverable is a sky spectrum, so we judge the transfer in flux space, on
reconstructed spectra. Coefficient errors can mislead: the 358 mesospheric
coefficients dominate score-space metrics, which can even disagree in sign
with flux-space metrics on the same predictions. We compare the residual
$y_{\rm sci} - \hat y_{\rm sci}$ of the predicted sky with three references.
The first is the decomposition's own fit to $y_{\rm sci}$, which is the
floor: the transfer predicts the coefficients of that fit, so it cannot
describe the spectrum better. The second is the single-fiber photon noise
(Section~\ref{sec:noise}), and the third is the sky itself. All numbers below
refer to the 2852 held-out observations.

Figure~\ref{fig:chi2-heldout} compares the prediction with the first two
references. Before the corrections of Section~\ref{sec:postcorr}, the median
$\chi^2$ of the prediction over the full band is 1.12, against 0.77 for the
decomposition's own fit, a ratio of 1.46; in the blue the medians are 0.54
and 0.40, a ratio of 1.34. The typical residual of the predicted sky is thus
1.06 times the single-fiber noise per pixel over the full band and 0.73
times it in the blue, where the decomposition alone reaches 0.88 and 0.64.
If the transfer error adds in quadrature to the decomposition residual, it
amounts to about 0.59 of the single-fiber noise over the full band and 0.37
in the blue, or about 5\% and 3.5\% of the sky per pixel. The prediction
$\chi^2$ is below 1 for 42\% of the observations over the full band and for
78\% in the blue.

Both distributions peak below $\chi^2=1$, but that of the uncorrected
prediction sits at higher values and has a longer tail, with an 84th
percentile of 2.32 over the full band and 1.22 in the blue. The far tail
consists mostly of observations that the decomposition itself fits poorly,
so their truth is not reliable either. After the two corrections of
Section~\ref{sec:postcorr}, the prediction moves below the decomposition:
the median falls to 0.31 over the full band and 0.21 in the blue, and 83\%
and 87\% of the observations end below $\chi^2=1$.

Figure~\ref{fig:residual-heldout} shows where in wavelength the error lies,
as percentile bands over the held-out observations at each pixel. The
second and third panels show the sky-subtracted science spectrum, observed
minus predicted, with and without the corrections, and the top row enlarges
two windows of the corrected one. We leave out the science-line windows
(Section~\ref{sec:noise}), where the residual is the nebular emission of the
science fields that sky subtraction exists to reveal; the narrow peaks at
H$\epsilon$ and H$\delta$, which the windows do not cover, are the same
emission. Without the corrections, the median residual departs from zero by
0.31 times the single-fiber noise at a typical pixel. It is structured in
the blue, where it traces the solar-line pattern of
Section~\ref{sec:armcorr}, and along the OH bands, and its 68\% range lies
within the single-fiber noise on only 33\% of the pixels. With the
corrections the median stays within 0.04 times the noise of zero at a
typical pixel, and the 68\% range lies within the noise on 96\% of the
pixels (98\% below 5000\,\AA). The 95\% range is wider than the noise
everywhere and widest in the far blue, with a half-width of 0.6 below
4000\,\AA{} against 0.2 in the red. Because the corrections act on narrow
features, they barely change the broad level of a spectrum: the median
residual of an observation is typically 2.0\% of its sky level (5.8\% at the
84th percentile), and over the band the predicted sky matches the observed
flux to 1.5\% at the median and 3.9\% at the 84th percentile, with or
without them.

The three lower panels isolate the transfer, comparing the predicted
continuum components with the decomposition of the science spectrum. For
all three the median is zero to within 2\% of the single-fiber noise, and
the 68\% range lies within the noise at every wavelength. The moonlight
error dominates and grows toward the blue, where scattered moonlight is
brightest. Near 4000\,\AA, where the median continuum with the Moon up is 5
in these units, its 68\% range is $\pm0.10$ and its 95\% range $-0.54$ to
$+0.68$, against a single-fiber noise of 0.22. The 68\% ranges of the
zodiacal and diffuse errors are typically $\pm0.02$ and $\pm0.01$, and their
95\% ranges $\pm0.09$ and $\pm0.035$.

The same comparison over the whole predicted sky, split into the 1101
observations with the Moon down ($f_{\rm pred}\le1/150$) and the 1751 with
the Moon up, leads to the same conclusion. The median transfer error is
typically 3\% and 2\% of the single-fiber noise in the two regimes (0.3\% of
the sky in the blue, 0.14\% in the near-infrared), and its 68\% range lies
inside the noise on 97\% of the pixels. The exceptions are the bright lines,
[O\,\textsc{i}]\,5577 and 6300, Na\,\textsc{i}\,D and the OH bands beyond
7300\,\AA, where a small fractional error in a line amplitude is still large
in flux. In dark time the 95\% range is skewed to positive values, so the
largest dark-time errors are under-predictions of the sky.

\subsection{Prediction}

At prediction time the inputs are the near and far spectra of an exposure,
their decompositions, and the context of all three arms. We normalise the
sky coefficients (Section~\ref{sec:normalisation}) and transform them to
scores; each ensemble member predicts the science scores with
Equation~(\ref{eq:output}), and we invert the scores to coefficients and
multiply them by the science-arm geometry factors. We then apply the rules
of Section~\ref{sec:rules} and the calibration of
Section~\ref{sec:calibration}, average the members, and reconstruct the
science sky spectrum with the science spectrum's LSF and transmission.
Finally we add the two empirical corrections of Section~\ref{sec:postcorr}.

Each prediction carries a reliability mask in the vocabulary of
Table~\ref{tab:flags}. We evaluate diffuse collapse from the predicted
coefficients, moon/zodiacal reversal from the reconstructed continua, and
the degenerate-continuum flag from the sky arms. The constraint warnings do
not apply to a prediction, since they describe how the decomposition shaped
the training targets.

Figures~\ref{fig:pipeline-moon} and~\ref{fig:pipeline-zodi} follow two
observations through the whole procedure: the decompositions of the two sky
arms, the predicted science sky with its components, and the delivered
sky-subtracted spectrum. In the first, with the Moon up, moonlight dominates
the continuum in both arms, and the two arms and the prediction look alike.
The predicted sky follows the observed science spectrum closely: the
residual stays near zero across the band, and its largest excursions lie in
the far blue and among the strongest OH lines beyond 9000\,\AA, where the
decomposition itself leaves residuals (Figure~\ref{fig:example-decomp-up}).
We chose the second observation to test the transfer on a large difference
between the arms. The Moon is far below the horizon
($h_{\rm moon}=-32.9^\circ$, illumination 0.01), so the moonlight of both
arms sits at its floor, and the difference between the arms comes from
zodiacal light, whose brightness depends on ecliptic latitude. The near and
far arms differ by 0.36\,dex (a factor of 2.3) in median flux and by
0.49\,dex (a factor of 3.1) in zodiacal-light amplitude, so the transfer has
to decide from context alone how much of each to carry into the science
pointing. The residual is again flat in the continuum. Its many spikes are
the nebular emission of this science field, which the sky subtraction
reveals: the Balmer lines, [O\,\textsc{ii}], [O\,\textsc{iii}],
[N\,\textsc{ii}], [S\,\textsc{ii}] and [S\,\textsc{iii}] 9069, 9531. The
decomposition also finds this science spectrum harder to fit than the first,
with a fit $\chi^2$ about six times higher.

\section{Empirical post-prediction corrections}
\label{sec:postcorr}

Because the transfer predicts the coefficients of the science spectrum's
decomposition, the predicted sky inherits whatever that decomposition cannot
describe. Two parts of that residual are large, systematic, and measurable
at the time of the exposure: a fixed pattern of solar absorption lines in the
moonlight and zodiacal-light continuum, and the brightness of the sky lines.
We correct both after the prediction. For the first we use the residuals of
the two sky arms against their own decompositions; for the second we fit the
brightness of the predicted sky lines on the spectrum being sky-subtracted.
Neither changes the decomposition or the network. Each adds a term to the
predicted sky,
\begin{equation}
  \hat y_{\rm sci}' = \hat y_{\rm sci} + \delta_{\rm arm} + \delta_{\rm line},
  \label{eq:postcorr}
\end{equation}
and we fit the second after the first. All $\chi^2$ values in this section
are medians over the held-out observations, with the science-line windows
excluded.

Both corrections take the predicted sky below the decomposition's own fit,
the floor of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}. That is no contradiction: the
floor is the best the basis of Section~\ref{sec:model} can do, and both
corrections bring in information from outside the basis, the simultaneous
residuals of the sky arms and the science spectrum itself.
Figures~\ref{fig:chi2-heldout} and~\ref{fig:residual-heldout} show the
combined effect on the 2852 held-out observations, with both corrections
applied over the full band. The median $\chi^2$ falls from 1.12 to 0.31
over the full band and from 0.54 to 0.21 in the blue. The corrected
prediction fits 80\% of the observations better than their own
decomposition over the full band and 74\% in the blue, and it is worse than
the uncorrected prediction on 0.3\% and 1.2\% of them.

\subsection{Sky-arm residual correction}
\label{sec:armcorr}

In the blue, one pattern of solar absorption lines dominates the residual of
every decomposition: the Balmer lines, Ca\,\textsc{ii} H and K and the G
band. The solar spectrum is in the basis (Section~\ref{sec:moonfam}), so
the pattern is a mismatch of its lines rather than their absence. A
wavelength shift explains none of it. The observed lines are broader than
the model's and about 21\% shallower, as the filling-in of Fraunhofer lines
by Raman scattering would produce. The reference blue LSF of
Section~\ref{sec:lsf} removes much of the width mismatch: on 2000
observations, the fractional residual of the brighter half falls from 5.0\%
to 2.2\% of the solar part of the model, $S$ (the sum of moonlight and
zodiacal light), and the median blue $\chi^2$ of the science decomposition
from 1.21 to 0.61. The rest of the pattern remains.

The two sky arms carry the same pattern at the same instant, so their
residuals measure it directly. For arm $a$ (near or far), with observed
spectrum $y_a$ and its own reconstruction $m_a$, we estimate the pattern in
the science spectrum as
\begin{equation}
  e_a(\lambda) = k_a(\lambda)\,\big[y_a(\lambda) - m_a(\lambda)\big],
  \qquad
  k_a = \frac{\hat m_{\rm sci}}{m_a},
  \label{eq:arm-estimate}
\end{equation}
where $\hat m_{\rm sci}$ is the predicted science sky. We take the ratio
pixel by pixel over the whole model and clip it to $[0.2, 5]$. The residual
at a pixel scales with whatever light makes up that pixel, sunlight in the
continuum, airglow in the diffuse emission, OH in a line core, and the ratio
of the total models follows each of them without a choice of family. On
OH-line pixels in particular the residual scales with the OH rather than
with the sunlight, and there this ratio does clearly better than one of the
solar components alone. We combine the two arms with inverse-variance
weights,
\begin{equation}
  \delta_{\rm arm} = \frac{\sum_a e_a/\sigma_a^2}{\sum_a 1/\sigma_a^2},
  \qquad
  \sigma_a^2 = k_a^2\operatorname{var}(y_a),
\end{equation}
with the photon variance of each arm's median spectrum
(Section~\ref{sec:noise}).

Three safeguards turned out to be necessary. An arm whose own decomposition
failed carries a residual unrelated to the science spectrum, so we drop an
arm when the RMS of $(y_a-m_a)/S_a$ below 5000\,\AA{} exceeds 0.25; the
median is 0.06, and failed fits sit near 1. Without this gate, a single
failed far-arm fit raised the mean $\chi^2$ forty-fold. On faint
observations the arm noise competes with the pattern. A running median over
three pixels helps there but smooths away the pattern on bright
observations, so we apply it only where the solar continuum is faint, at a
signal-to-noise ratio below 100 against the combined arm noise. Finally, a
narrow feature in one sky field, such as nebular emission, a star or a
cosmic ray, is not part of the shared pattern, yet the weighted mean would
carry about half of it into the science spectrum. Where the two arms' scaled
residuals differ by more than five times their combined photon noise, we
therefore take the arm with the smaller residual instead of the mean. This
affects 1.8\% of the pixels. For a 2.5\,\AA-wide line injected into the near
arm only, at 0.3, 1 and 3 times the local continuum, it lowers the fraction
transferred to the science from 48\% to 7, 1.4 and 0.3\%, while the median
blue $\chi^2$ of the corrected science decomposition rises only from 0.129
to 0.138. Smoothing does much worse at the same task: an 8\,\AA{} running
median still transfers 15--25\% of such a line and doubles that $\chi^2$. A
feature that appears in both sky fields passes, since it is then
indistinguishable from the shared sky pattern. This includes diffuse
nebular emission common to both fields, which we therefore suppress at the
wavelengths of the nebular lines (Section~\ref{sec:lineprotect}).

Restricted to $\lambda<5000$\,\AA, as in Table~\ref{tab:armcorr}, the
correction ends with a cosine taper over the following 100\,\AA{} rather
than a step. The taper lies outside the corrected range on purpose, because
one ending at 5000\,\AA{} would lose about half of the gain over the last
100\,\AA{} of the range. The correction works equally well redward of
5000\,\AA, and we apply it over the whole band, as in the evaluation above
and in the sky subtraction of the reduced frames
(Section~\ref{sec:fullband}).

Table~\ref{tab:armcorr} summarises the effect on the 2852 held-out
observations. Below 5000\,\AA{} the median $\chi^2$ falls from 0.63 to 0.23,
and 0.9\% of the observations end up worse than uncorrected. The near arm
alone, unscaled and unsmoothed, reaches 0.32 and makes 18\% of the
observations worse. The two arms beat it on 82\% of the observations;
relative to it they lower the $\chi^2$ by a factor of 0.61 in the faintest
quartile of the moonlight, where their noise averages down, and by 10\% in
the brightest. A 10\,\AA{} median of the near arm keeps most of its gain
(0.36), since the pattern is broad on average, but it also transfers narrow
features of that sky field. The gain is smaller in crowded fields, where
part of the residual is the science target's own light. Over the full band,
with the correction applied everywhere, the median $\chi^2$ falls from 1.12
to 0.41, and 0.1\% of the observations get worse.

\subsection{Sky-line scaling}
\label{sec:linescale}

The transfer reproduces the shapes of the sky lines well, but not their
brightness. OH emission differs by a few percent between the science and sky
directions and across the field of view, and this is most of the flux error
that remains. A sky line, however, is two to three \AA{} wide and stands out
from whatever science signal lies under it. We can therefore measure its
brightness on the spectrum it is subtracted from, while leaving alone the
broad science continuum, which the sky model cannot separate from the
target.

We split the line emission of the predicted sky into a few templates $T_b$,
built from the predicted coefficients on the reconstruction's own basis, so
that they sum to its line components. Each OH coefficient is the population
of one upper level (Table~\ref{tab:families}), so the OH coefficients group
naturally by vibrational level. Each band $v'=3,\dots,9$ gets one template,
with $v'=10$, under 1\% of the OH flux, merged into $v'=9$. Each band also
gets a second template, its lines weighted by
$(E_{\rm up}-\langle E_{\rm up}\rangle)/1000\,{\rm cm}^{-1}$; since
rotational populations follow $e^{-E_{\rm up}/kT}$, this term absorbs a
difference in rotational temperature to first order. Each atomic line family
and the O$_2$ band get one template each. On the science spectrum $y$, we
model the residual after the sky-arm correction as
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal H\big[y - \hat m_{\rm sci} - \delta_{\rm arm}\big]
  = \sum_b d_b\,\mathcal H\big[T_b\big] + \epsilon ,
  \label{eq:linescale}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal H[x] = x - \langle x\rangle_{25\,\text{\AA}}$ removes a
running mean over 25\,\AA. We apply this linear high-pass identically to the
data and the templates. The broad science continuum disappears, and what the
running mean takes from the lines it takes equally from the templates, so
Equation~(\ref{eq:linescale}) remains exact. We fit the fractional
brightness changes $d_b$ by weighted least squares, with the photon variance
plus a science term as the variance (Section~\ref{sec:sciencesafe}), the
science-line windows masked, and a Gaussian prior $d_b\sim\mathcal N(0,
0.1^2)$ that keeps a template with little signal at the prediction. The fit
iterates Huber weights on the standardised residuals (threshold 2), so that
narrow science features, stellar absorption lines and unmasked emission
lines are down-weighted instead of pulling the scales. The correction is
$\delta_{\rm line}=\sum_b d_b\,T_b$, with the unfiltered templates.

Four families keep their predicted brightness: Na\,\textsc{i}\,D,
K\,\textsc{i}, [O\,\textsc{i}]\,6300/6364 and N\,\textsc{i}\,5199. Each
coincides with a science feature that the fit cannot tell apart from the sky
line (Section~\ref{sec:sciencesafe}).

In production we fit each fiber, so that the correction also absorbs
variations of the sky lines across the IFU and differences in fiber
throughput. Here we evaluate it on the median science spectra of the 2852
held-out observations, after a full-band sky-arm correction
(Table~\ref{tab:linescale}). In the red the median $\chi^2$ falls from 0.51
to 0.35, and on the OH-line pixels from 1.11 to 0.32, against 0.86 for the
decomposition's own fit. We fit the scales on the high-passed lines, but the
correction also carries the broad part of the templates, the line wings and
blends, which it needs on average. On 8.7\% of the observations the $\chi^2$
in the red nevertheless rises, by at most 9\%. This is not damage to the
lines. These observations carry a broad offset between the lines, continuum
light mostly in crowded fields, which meets the small, opposite-signed broad
part of the correction. Once we remove broad offsets from the residual with
a 100\,\AA{} high-pass, the median $\chi^2$ in the red falls from 0.49 to
0.20, and only 1.5\% of the observations are worse. On an earlier version of
the decomposition we also checked the fitted scales against the truth, the
band brightness of the science spectrum's own decomposition relative to the
prediction: the fit recovered it to 0.1--0.6\% per band, where the
prediction alone missed by 1.5--2.7\%. The result barely depended on the
high-pass width (10--50\,\AA), the prior width (0.03--0.3) or the Huber
weights, and a single scale for all OH bands did clearly worse than one per
band.

\subsubsection{Protecting the science signal}
\label{sec:sciencesafe}

Because we fit the line scaling on the spectrum it corrects, any science
signal that projects onto the sky-line templates leaks into the sky model and
is subtracted from the science. We tested this by injecting signal into 120
uncrowded held-out observations of the earlier version of the
decomposition. The stars were PHOENIX spectra \citep{Husser2013} of a G2
dwarf, K3 dwarf and giant, and M2 dwarf and M3 giant, at 0.3 to 10 times the
sky continuum and radial velocities within $\pm150$\,km\,s$^{-1}$. The lines
were 15 red nebular lines at 0.5 and 3 times the sky continuum (He\,\textsc{i},
[O\,\textsc{i}], [Ar\,\textsc{iii}], [O\,\textsc{ii}]\,7320/7330,
[S\,\textsc{iii}]\,9069/9531 and the Paschen series), with H$\alpha$,
[N\,\textsc{ii}] and [S\,\textsc{ii}] at a common velocity within
$\pm300$\,km\,s$^{-1}$. We define the leak as the change of the correction
caused by the injected signal. Four measures turned out to be necessary.

First, families that coincide with a science feature keep their predicted
brightness. Science [O\,\textsc{i}]\,6300/6364 falls on the sky line and,
when rescaled, lost up to all of its flux. Na\,\textsc{i}\,D and
K\,\textsc{i} are strong stellar absorption lines: with a solar spectrum at
five times the sky continuum, rescaling Na\,D raised the $\chi^2$ in its
window from 0.16 to 5.3. N\,\textsc{i}\,5199, 0.1\% of the line flux, sits on
the stellar Mg\,b lines, and its scale moved by up to 0.5 under a bright
star.

Second, the decomposition's science-line windows (Section~\ref{sec:noise})
cover only the blue lines, H$\alpha$, [N\,\textsc{ii}] and
[S\,\textsc{ii}]. For the line scaling we mask the red nebular lines as
well, slid by the measured H$\alpha$ velocity, and widen the windows by
$\pm150$\,km\,s$^{-1}$ when H$\alpha$ is too faint to give one. Without the
extension, faint lines that fall on OH lines lost 1--2\% of their flux at
the median and up to 8\%.

Third, a star's absorption lines and band heads are as narrow as the sky
lines, so they survive any high-pass. We therefore treat them as noise,
adding 10\% of the local science continuum, the running median of the
residual over 100\,\AA, in quadrature to the photon noise. This lowers the
stellar leak by about 40\% at ten times the sky continuum.

Fourth, we apply no $\chi^2$ guard, that is, we do not drop the correction
when it raises the $\chi^2$ of the spectrum. That $\chi^2$ includes the
science continuum, and a star of 0.3--10 times the sky continuum reversed
such a decision on 40--75\% of the observations.

Table~\ref{tab:sciencesafe} gives the result. A faint nebular line loses at
most 0.6\% of its flux at the median, and at most 4\% at the 5th percentile;
most of that comes from lines whose H$\alpha$ velocity could not be measured
and which lie beyond the widened windows. A strong line loses at most
0.2\%. A star alters the correction by 0.14--0.6\% of its own flux in the
red, and the OH scales by at most 0.03 at the sky continuum level and 0.16
at ten times it. These protections cost 0.02 in the median $\chi^2$ in the
red, mostly because the sky [O\,\textsc{i}] lines are no longer rescaled.

A brightness scale cannot repair a wrong line shape, so the correction
depends on the LSF of the reconstruction (Section~\ref{sec:lsf}). The
held-out reconstructions use the LSF surfaces fitted by the decomposition.
When we reconstructed a test exposure with the nominal, Gaussian LSF of the
reduced frame instead, the residual at the line cores was about 20\% of the
line peak, and the scaling gained nothing; with the LSF surface fitted to the
near arm, the RMS residual at the line cores fell from $2.2\times10^{-15}$
to $1.1\times10^{-15}$\,erg\,s$^{-1}$\,cm$^{-2}$\,\AA$^{-1}$. The science
fibers therefore need a fitted LSF for this correction to reach its
held-out performance.
