\section{Problem statement}
\label{sec:problem}

LVM \citep{Drory2024} observes extended, low-surface-brightness emission from
the Milky Way and the Magellanic Clouds, for which the night sky is a
first-order contaminant. Each science exposure is accompanied by two
simultaneous sky exposures taken with dedicated sky telescopes pointed at
blank fields a few degrees from the science field. The sky is not uniform on
this baseline. Scattered moonlight has strong angular structure, set by the
scattering phase functions of molecules and aerosols. The zodiacal light
varies by tens of percent with ecliptic latitude and solar elongation across
the pointings of one night. OH airglow is modulated by mesospheric gravity
waves on scales of tens of arcminutes. The three lines of sight also have
different zenith angles, and so different slant paths through the emitting
layers and the atmosphere. Subtracting either sky spectrum directly therefore
leaves systematic residuals of one to ten percent of the sky, enough to bury
the diffuse emission that motivates the survey.

The procedure described here replaces direct subtraction by a prediction.
Each spectrum is first decomposed into the contributions of physically
distinct emitters (Section~\ref{sec:decomp}). These contributions are
converted to a zenith-equivalent frame that removes the known effect of
viewing geometry (Section~\ref{sec:geometry}). A neural network, which we
call the \emph{transfer}, then predicts the contributions at the science
pointing from those at the two sky pointings and from the observing geometry
(Section~\ref{sec:ml}). The prediction is turned back into a sky spectrum on
the native pixel grid and subtracted from the science spectrum.

The natural yardstick for the result is the photon noise of a single fiber,
because the sky model is subtracted from individual fibers. On observations
not used in training, the predicted sky reaches a median reduced $\chi^2$ of
1.27 against that noise, where the decomposition's own fit to the same
spectra reaches 0.92; the prediction thus adds an error of about 0.6 times
the single-fiber noise, or about 5\% of the sky per pixel
(Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}).

\section{Input data}
\label{sec:inputs}

\paragraph{Instrument.} The LVM instrument comprises separate telescopes for
science, for two sky fields (east and west) and for spectrophotometric
standards, all feeding fiber bundles into spectrographs with three channels
($b$, $r$, $z$) covering $3600$--$9800$\,\AA. The fiber diameter is
$35.3''$. We call the three pointings of an exposure its \emph{arms}: the
science arm and two sky arms. The sky arm closer to the science field on the
sky is the \emph{near} arm and the other the \emph{far} arm. This assignment
changes from exposure to exposure and does not depend on which physical
telescope is used.

\paragraph{Spectra.} The inputs are the reduced, flux-calibrated frames of
the LVM data reduction pipeline (DRP). The DRP divides every fiber by a model
telluric transmission evaluated along the \emph{science} line of sight, which
matters for the decomposition (Section~\ref{sec:telluric}). For each exposure
we form three spectra on a common grid of $N_\lambda = 12\,401$ pixels at
$\Delta\lambda = 0.5$\,\AA: the median over the fibers of each sky telescope,
and the median over the science fibers after rejecting fibers whose Gaia DR3
\citep{GaiaDR3} predicted stellar $G$-band flux exceeds 1\% of the fiber's
measured flux plus sky. The median is a robust estimate of the
sky-dominated spectrum, with the variance of a mean over
$N_{\rm eff} = (2/\pi)\,n_{\rm fib}$ fibers, where $n_{\rm fib}$ is the
number of fibers combined. Each spectrum carries the per-pixel line-spread
function (LSF) width of its fiber bundle.

\paragraph{Metadata.} Each exposure provides the pointing coordinates and
airmass of all three telescopes, the exposure mid-time and duration, the
precipitable water vapour (PWV) used by the DRP, and which sky telescope is
near and which far. From these we compute the solar, lunar and ecliptic
geometry of each pointing and look up solar and geomagnetic activity indices
for the date (Section~\ref{sec:ctx}).

\paragraph{Data set.} The data set contains $18\,668$ exposures. We call one
exposure with its three spectra (science, near, far) an \emph{observation}.
$13\,688$ observations survive the selection of Section~\ref{sec:selection}.

\section{Physical decomposition}
\label{sec:decomp}

Each of the three spectra of an observation is fitted independently by the
same physical model. The fit returns a non-negative vector of $P = 388$
coefficients, one per template spectrum, together with their uncertainties,
the spectrum of each emitter and a set of reliability flags. The transfer of
Section~\ref{sec:ml} works on these coefficients, so the decomposition is
designed to give each coefficient a fixed physical meaning from one
observation to the next, even where the data alone could not enforce it.

\subsection{Model}
\label{sec:model}

The observed spectrum is written as a non-negative linear combination of
templates:
\begin{equation}
  y(\lambda) \;\simeq\; \sum_{k=1}^{P} c_k\,T_k(\lambda), \qquad c_k \ge 0 ,
  \label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
where each template $T_k$ is a physical spectrum convolved with the LSF
fitted to the same spectrum (Section~\ref{sec:lsf}), attenuated by the
atmosphere as appropriate for its emitting layer
(Section~\ref{sec:telluric}), and divided by the transmission the DRP
removed. Stacking the templates gives the design matrix
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  \mathbf{A} = \big[\,&\mathbf{A}_{\rm OH}\,\big|\,\mathbf{A}_{\rm O_2}\,\big|\,
  \mathbf{A}_{\rm atom}\,\big|\\
  &\mathbf{A}_{\rm moon}\,\big|\,\mathbf{A}_{\rm zodi}\,\big|\,
  \mathbf{A}_{\rm diff}\,\big]
  \in \mathbb{R}^{N_\lambda\times P}.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
The templates are grouped into \emph{families}, one per emitter
(Table~\ref{tab:families}). The families divide into a \emph{line} block
(OH, O$_2$, atomic lines), which depends on the LSF, and a \emph{continuum}
block (moonlight, zodiacal light, diffuse airglow), which is smooth on the
scale of the LSF. This division structures the estimation
(Section~\ref{sec:estimation}).

The \emph{amplitude} of a family $g$ is the flux it contributes, integrated
over wavelength,
\begin{equation}
  A_g = \vect{c}_g\cdot\vect{v}_g ,
  \label{eq:amplitude}
\end{equation}
where $\vect c_g$ holds the family's coefficients and $\vect v_g$ the
wavelength integrals of its templates. The integrals are taken before
atmospheric attenuation (Section~\ref{sec:telluric}), so $A_g$ measures the
emission itself. For moonlight and zodiacal light the coefficients are
spline weights that also set the spectral shape, and the amplitude is their
total flux.

\subsection{Line-spread function}
\label{sec:lsf}

Every narrow template depends on the LSF, which varies with wavelength and
between spectrograph channels. We represent the LSF kernel at wavelength
$\lambda$ as a continuous probability density in the offset
$\delta = \lambda' - \lambda$:
\begin{equation}
  K(\delta;\lambda) = \sum_{j=1}^{11} a_j(\lambda)\,M_j(\delta),
  \qquad \sum_j a_j(\lambda) = 1 .
  \label{eq:lsf}
\end{equation}
Here the $M_j$ are cubic M-splines on equally spaced knots over
$|\delta|\le3$\,\AA, each normalised
to unit integral, so $K$ integrates to one. The fit constrains the kernel to
be non-negative and unimodal, with a single central peak, at every
wavelength. The weights vary smoothly with wavelength within each channel: they are constant
in the $b$ channel and a six-function cubic B-spline in $\lambda$ in the $r$
and $z$ channels. A line template is rendered by integrating
$K(\,\cdot\,;\lambda_\ell)$ analytically over each pixel for every line
$\ell$ of the family. The LSF parameters are fitted to the sky lines of the
spectrum itself (Section~\ref{sec:estimation}), with a roughness penalty on the
weights and a low-order polynomial nuisance background in each channel. A
Gaussian of the DRP-reported width seeds the fit, and it is retained for any
channel that carries too little line information to constrain the kernel. The same fitted LSF
convolves the continuum templates, where it has little effect.

\subsection{Atmospheric transmission}
\label{sec:telluric}

The airglow is emitted at 85--300\,km and reaches the telescope through the
whole lower atmosphere. The OH Meinel bands in the red and near-infrared
overlap the H$_2$O and O$_2$ absorption bands. If undimmed templates were
fitted, the line amplitudes would absorb the transmission. That transmission
depends on airmass and water vapour, so it would not cancel between the
pointings, and the transfer would be asked to predict atmospheric absorption
disguised as mesospheric emission. Two corrections avoid this. First, each
line template is attenuated at its own centre wavelength by the PALACE
molecular transmission, evaluated from high-resolution line optical depths at
the airmass $X_{\rm src}$ of the fiber being fitted,
\begin{equation}
  t_\ell = \exp\!\Big\{-X_{\rm src}\big[\tau_\ell^{\rm dry}
  + (w/w_0)\,\tau_\ell^{\rm H_2O}\big]\Big\},
\end{equation}
where $w$ is the PWV and $w_0 = 2.5$\,mm is the reference water column.
Second, every template is divided by the smooth transmission
$T_{\rm DRP}(\lambda;\,X_{\rm sci},w)$ that the DRP removed from all fibers
along the science line of sight, so that model and data are compared in the
frame in which the data are delivered. The design matrix of a spectrum is
therefore $\mathbf{A}^{\rm obs} = \mathbf{A}(X_{\rm src}, w)/T_{\rm
DRP}(\lambda; X_{\rm sci}, w)$, and the coefficients keep their meaning as
top-of-atmosphere amplitudes of the emitting layer.

Here and throughout, $\chi^2$ is the reduced $\chi^2$ of the fit residual
under the photon-noise model of Section~\ref{sec:noise}, and \emph{the blue}
is the range blueward of 6000\,\AA, where the continuum rather than OH
dominates the sky. Including the transmission lowers the decomposition
$\chi^2$ by about 30\% over the full band, with essentially no change in the
blue, which has no telluric absorption.

\subsection{OH and O$_2$ airglow}
\label{sec:oh}

OH nightglow dominates the sky beyond about 6000\,\AA, and the LVM
resolution separates individual rotational transitions. The model contains
357 OH templates. Each is the sum of the PALACE \citep{Noll2025} transitions
that share $(v', N', F')$, with relative strengths taken from a population
model. Grouping by upper state lets the fit follow changes in the rotational
and vibrational populations, which trace mesospheric temperature and
chemistry, while keeping the number of free parameters well below the number
of transitions.

\paragraph{Ridge-corrected line strengths.} The relative line strengths
within each group are a linear correction of the PALACE population model.
The correction was fitted on far-arm sky spectra with ridge regularisation
($\lambda_{\rm ridge} = 0.1$). The correction mainly repairs lines in the
$r$ channel. There the canonical relative strengths are too weak by about
0.11\,dex. The fit could not compensate, because those lines share
coefficients with the brighter $z$-channel lines of the same upper state. On
1000 test observations the correction reduces the decomposition $\chi^2$ on
OH-dominated pixels by factors of 33 in $r$ and 3.6 in $z$, and by 31\% over
the full band, with every observation improving. The systematic residual at
the OH lines, stacked over observations, falls from 1.1\% to 0.14\% of the
line peak in $r$. The coefficients themselves barely change (0.8\% at the
90th percentile), nor do the continuum partition and the reliability flags.
Because the coefficients hardly change, any reconstruction must use the same
line strengths as the fit: a mismatch is invisible in the coefficients and
appears only in the reconstructed spectrum.

\paragraph{O$_2$ atmospheric band.} The O$_2$ $b$-band near 8645\,\AA{} is
first fitted on its own. The band shape found for each spectrum then enters
the main fit as a single template with one free amplitude.

\subsection{Atomic lines}

Seven atomic features enter as single templates. The mesopause lines are
K\,\textsc{i} 7699, Na\,\textsc{i}\,D and [O\,\textsc{i}] 5577. The
thermospheric (F-region) lines are N\,\textsc{i} 5199,
[O\,\textsc{i}] 6300/6364 and the O\,\textsc{i} 7774 and 8446 recombination
lines. The distinction matters for the geometric normalisation
(Section~\ref{sec:heights}).

\subsection{Scattered moonlight}
\label{sec:moonfam}

Scattered moonlight is the dominant broadband contaminant with the Moon up.
Its template family is
\begin{equation}
  T_{{\rm moon},j}(\lambda) = I_\odot(\lambda)\;
  \alpha_{\rm ROLO}(\lambda,\phi_0)\; B^{(11)}_j(\lambda),
\end{equation}
for $j = 1,\dots,15$, where $I_\odot$ is the solar spectrum
\citep{Meftah2018}, $\alpha_{\rm ROLO}$ the disk-integrated lunar albedo
\citep{Kieffer2005} at a fiducial phase angle $\phi_0 = 30^\circ$, and
$B^{(11)}_j$ are cubic B-splines with 11 interior knots across the band.
Their weighted sum, the spline \emph{multiplier}, absorbs the wavelength
dependence of atmospheric
scattering and the departure of the actual lunar phase from $\phi_0$. The
albedo carries the lunar mineral absorption bands, which give the family a
distinctive shape. At this knot spacing no adjacent pair of basis functions
is correlated above $|\rho| = 0.99$.

\subsection{Zodiacal light}
\label{sec:zodifam}

The zodiacal light is sunlight scattered by interplanetary dust, slightly
reddened. Its family is
\begin{equation}
  T_{{\rm zodi},j}(\lambda) = I_\odot(\lambda)\,
  \left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_0}\right)^{0.26} B^{(1)}_j(\lambda),
\end{equation}
for $j = 1,\dots,5$, with $\lambda_0 = 5000$\,\AA{} and a single interior
knot. The multiplier is further restricted by a
curvature penalty (Section~\ref{sec:qp}), so it is nearly monotone. Moonlight
and zodiacal light must be separate families: otherwise the smooth zodiacal
continuum is absorbed into the moon family on dark, low-ecliptic-latitude
observations. The moon coefficients then have a mixed identity that no
transfer can learn, because the two emitters depend on disjoint geometries.

\subsection{Diffuse airglow continuum}
\label{sec:diffuse}

Three fixed templates describe the broadband airglow continuum, following the
PALACE components \citep{Noll2024,Noll2025}. HO$_2$ and FeO (the ``orange
arc'') are the PALACE templates, interpolated onto the LVM grid. The O$_2$
continuum was re-derived on LVM sky spectra: the canonical template peaks at
3220\,\AA, outside the LVM band, so only its tail is constrained, and over
$4200$--$5900$\,\AA{} it runs about twice too high and biases the blue
residual. With the canonical HO$_2$/FeO pair, FeO peaks at 5966\,\AA, where FeO(VIS)
is expected, and the 580--610\,nm feature is attributed to FeO rather than
HO$_2$.

PALACE is calibrated at Cerro Paranal, while LVM observes from Las Campanas.
The absolute PALACE continuum levels are therefore indicative only. In dark
time (Moon below the horizon) the fitted diffuse block amounts to 7.8\% of the 500--720\,nm airglow,
against $3.3\pm0.8\%$ in the PALACE reference. This factor of 2.4 is not
removed by any basis choice or constraint considered here. Either the
continuum at Las Campanas differs, or the block contains emission that is not
airglow.

\subsection{Physical model of the moonlight and zodiacal-light brightness}
\label{sec:mzmodel}

The identifiability constraints (Section~\ref{sec:constraints}) and three of
the transfer's context features (Section~\ref{sec:ctx}) need an absolute,
geometry-driven prediction of the moonlight and zodiacal-light spectra at a
given pointing. We use a single-scattering model evaluated at the exposure
mid-time.

\paragraph{Moonlight.} The illuminating spectrum is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  F_{\rm moon}(\lambda) = {}&I_\odot(\lambda;\,v_{\rm moon})\;
  \alpha_{\rm ROLO}(\lambda,\phi)\\
  &\times\Big(\frac{R_{\rm moon}}{d_{\rm moon}}\Big)^{2}
  \Big(\frac{1\,{\rm au}}{d_{\odot,\rm moon}}\Big)^{2}.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
Here $I_\odot$ is Doppler-shifted by the velocity $v_{\rm moon}$ of the
reflected light, $\phi$ is the signed lunar phase angle, $R_{\rm moon}$ is
the lunar radius, $d_{\rm moon}$ is the Earth--Moon distance and
$d_{\odot,\rm moon}$ is the Sun--Moon distance. The scattered brightness at
a target of airmass $X_t$ and angular distance $\theta$ from the Moon is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  S_{\rm moon}(\lambda) = {}&H(h_{\rm moon})\,F_{\rm moon}(\lambda)\\
  &\times X_t\,e^{-\tau X_t}\,\Phi\big(\tau\,[X_{\rm moon}-X_t]\big)\\
  &\times\Big[\tau_{\rm R}\,P_{\rm R}(\theta) +
  0.97\,\tau_{\rm A}\,P_{\rm HG}(\theta)\Big]\,E(\lambda).
\end{split}
\end{equation}
Here $\tau = \tau_{\rm R}+\tau_{\rm A}$ is the sum of the Rayleigh optical
depth and the aerosol optical depth
$\tau_{\rm A} = 0.0336\,(\lambda/\lambda_0)^{-1.38}$;
$\Phi(\delta) = (1-e^{-\delta})/\delta$ is the two-airmass extinction factor;
$P_{\rm R}$ is the Rayleigh phase function with depolarisation factor 0.0148
and $P_{\rm HG}$ the Henyey--Greenstein phase function \citep{Henyey1941}
with $g = 0.8$; $E(\lambda) = [1+3.5\,X_t\,\tau_{\rm R}(\lambda)]/
[1+3.5\,X_t\,\tau_{\rm R}(\lambda_0)]$ approximates the multiple-scattering
enhancement of the blue; and $X_{\rm moon}$ is the airmass of the Moon.
Scattered moonlight persists with the Moon just below the horizon. It is
modelled by the taper $H = \exp[-8.22\,\tanh(\max(-h_{\rm moon},0)/5^\circ)]$,
which gives $-0.70$\,dex at $h_{\rm moon} = -1^\circ$, $-2.72$\,dex at
$-5^\circ$ and saturates near $-3.6$\,dex.

\paragraph{Zodiacal light.} The zodiacal brightness is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  S_{\rm zodi}(\lambda) = {}&B_{500}(\Delta\lambda_\odot,\beta)\;
  10^{\,f(\Delta\lambda_\odot,\,\beta)}\\
  &\times\frac{\tilde I_\odot(\lambda;\,v_{\rm z})}{I_\odot(\lambda_0)}
  \Big(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_0}\Big)^{0.26}
  e^{-X_t(\tau_{\rm R}+\tau_{\rm A})/2}.
\end{split}
  \label{eq:zodimodel}
\end{equation}
Here $B_{500}$ is the tabulated zodiacal brightness at 500\,nm
\citep{Leinert1998} as a function of the helio-ecliptic longitude
$\Delta\lambda_\odot \equiv |\lambda_{\rm ecl}-\lambda_\odot|$ and the
ecliptic latitude $\beta$, both geocentric; $\tilde I_\odot$ is the solar
spectrum broadened by 30\,km\,s$^{-1}$ and Doppler-shifted by the dust
velocity $v_{\rm z}$; the exponent 0.26 is the zodiacal reddening; the
factor $1/2$ in the extinction reflects forward scattering into the beam of
an extended source; and $f$ is the empirical correction described below.
Both spectra are multiplied by the fiber solid angle and projected onto the
LVM grid with the LSF of the spectrum. The constraints use two numbers from
the model:
the zodiacal integral $Z_{\rm pred} = \int S_{\rm zodi}\,\dd\lambda$ and the
moonlight fraction
\begin{equation}
  f_{\rm pred} = \frac{\int S_{\rm moon}\,\dd\lambda}
  {\int S_{\rm moon}\,\dd\lambda + \int S_{\rm zodi}\,\dd\lambda}.
\end{equation}
The ratio $f_{\rm pred}$ is independent of throughput and flux calibration,
because both components pass through the same conversion.

The model also has a calibrated version, in which the Rayleigh, aerosol and
zodiacal terms carry fitted scale factors that are exponential in
$\ln X_t$, $\ln X_{\rm moon}$, phase and $\cos\theta$. These factors were
fitted to the moon-plus-zodi \emph{sum} only, so the split between the two
components that they imply is not independently calibrated. The zodiacal
scale in particular grows with airmass as $X^{1.28}$, although extinction
should make it fall; this looks like absorbed moonlight. The constraints
therefore use the physics-only model, with all fitted scale factors set to
unity except the horizon taper. The two versions differ by a factor of
1.5--1.7 in absolute zodiacal light.

\paragraph{Empirical correction to the zodiacal profile.} In dark time
the fitted zodiacal amplitude is set by the data
(Section~\ref{sec:constraints}).
Comparing it with Equation~(\ref{eq:zodimodel}) with $f=0$ showed that the
tabulated profile falls off too steeply with ecliptic latitude for LVM: it is
too bright on the ecliptic and too faint toward the poles. It therefore
overstates the zodiacal contrast between neighbouring pointings. The slope of
fitted against modelled differences between the science and sky pointings
was only 0.45 (near) and 0.43 (far). We correct this with a multiplicative
term:
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
  f = \sum_{(i,j)} c_{ij}\,P_i(u)\,P_j(v),\\
  u = \frac{2\,\Delta\lambda_\odot}{180^\circ}-1, \qquad
  v = 2\sin|\beta| - 1,
\end{gathered}
  \label{eq:zodicorr}
\end{equation}
where the $P_i$ are Legendre polynomials of degree $\le 2$ and the
coefficients are listed in Table~\ref{tab:zodicorr}.

The correction was fitted on 14\,979 dark pointings, pooling all three arms;
pointings at a bound of the zodiacal bracket were treated as censored (a
Tobit likelihood; \citealt{Tobin1958}). The ecliptic shape was fitted
jointly with a Galactic-latitude term and a log-airmass term, but only the
ecliptic part is used. The zodiacal family also absorbs Galactic continuum
(diffuse Galactic light and unresolved stars, $-0.15$\,dex from the plane to
high latitude), for which the basis has no template, and the airmass term is
consistent with airglow leaking into the family; neither is zodiacal light.
With whole nights held out, the correction reduces the dark-time scatter of
fitted against predicted zodiacal light from 0.124 to 0.102\,dex and raises
the contrast slopes to 0.74 (near) and 0.94 (far). A degree-5 correction
fits absolute levels better but lowers the near-arm slope to 0.37, because
it fits structure that does not carry over between neighbouring pointings.
Over the observed pointings the correction ranges from $-0.13$ to
$+0.26$\,dex (median $+0.06$). The correction enters $Z_{\rm pred}$ and, through it, $f_{\rm pred}$, so the
two constraints that use them stay mutually consistent.

\subsection{Noise model and pixel weights}
\label{sec:noise}

The fit weights each pixel by the inverse of a photon-noise variance
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{var}(y_i) = \frac{y_i\,s(\lambda_i)}{t_{\rm exp}\,\Delta\lambda\,N_{\rm eff}},
\end{equation}
Here $s(\lambda)$ is the absolute spectral sensitivity of the instrument
(flux per detected count), $t_{\rm exp}$ is the exposure time and
$N_{\rm eff}$ is the effective number of fibers of the median. A floor of 5\%
of the median of the spectrum prevents the variance from vanishing. The
weights are normalised to unit mean over the good pixels of each spectrum.
This preserves the relative weighting across wavelength and leaves the
overall scale of the objective unchanged, so the regularisation strengths
keep their meaning. The red carries about three times the blue's weight: the
sky is brighter there, but the sensitivity is about seven times better, so
the variance is lower. Compared with uniform weights this lowers the $\chi^2$
by 11--14\% over the full band in all three arms, and by 2--4\% in the blue.

The same variance with $N_{\rm eff}=1$ is the photon noise of a single
fiber. It is the yardstick for the sky prediction
(Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}): the median spectra are cleaner than any one
fiber, but the sky model is subtracted from individual fibers, so the
residual of the model only matters in comparison with the single-fiber
noise. A $\chi^2$ of 1 against this noise means that the model error equals
it. For a typical observation, the single-fiber noise in a 0.5\,\AA{} pixel
is about 8\% of the sky flux over the full band and 9\% in the blue.

Pixels near the emission lines of the science target (Balmer lines, [N\,\textsc{ii}],
[S\,\textsc{ii}], [O\,\textsc{iii}] and others) carry no information about
the sky and are given zero weight. The windows are centred on the measured
H$\alpha$ velocity of the field and are masked in all three spectra of an
observation, so that the three fits use identical pixels. None of these
lines is in the basis; unmasked, they would be absorbed mostly into the OH
templates.

\subsection{Estimation}
\label{sec:estimation}

\subsubsection{Quadratic program}
\label{sec:qp}

For a given LSF the coefficients solve the regularised, weighted,
non-negative least-squares problem
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  \vect{c}^\star = \arg\min_{\substack{\vect{c}\ge 0\\ \mathbf{G}\vect{c}\le\vect{h}}}\;
  \Big[\,&\tfrac12\big\lVert \mathbf{W}^{1/2}(\mathbf{A}\vect{c}-\vect{y})\big\rVert_2^2\\
  &+ \lambda_{\rm m}\big\lVert \mathbf{D}^{(2)}\vect{c}_{\rm moon}\big\rVert_2^2
  + \lambda_{\rm z}\big\lVert \mathbf{D}^{(2)}\vect{c}_{\rm zodi}\big\rVert_2^2\Big].
\end{split}
  \label{eq:qp}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathbf{W}$ is the diagonal matrix of pixel weights
(Section~\ref{sec:noise}), $\mathbf{D}^{(2)}$ is the second-difference
operator on adjacent spline coefficients, with
$\lambda_{\rm m} = \lambda_{\rm z} = 0.1$ per unit of scaled flux, and
$\mathbf{G}\vect{c}\le\vect{h}$ collects the linear identifiability
constraints of Section~\ref{sec:constraints}. The problem is convex and is
solved with the interior-point conic solver Clarabel \citep{Goulart2024}.

Because the columns of $\mathbf{A}$ differ in scale by many orders of
magnitude, the problem is rescaled before the solve. The data are divided by
$d = \max(\mathrm{rms}(\mathbf{W}^{1/2}\vect{y}),1)$, each column $k$ by
$d\,s_k$ with $s_k = \lVert(\mathbf{W}^{1/2}\mathbf{A})_{:,k}\rVert_2$, and
the regularisation operators consistently,
$\mathbf{D}^{(2)}\to\mathbf{D}^{(2)}\,\mathrm{diag}(1/s)$. The solution is
transformed back afterwards. Constraints without a right-hand side are
unchanged by the rescaling; those with one are rescaled with the data.

\subsubsection{Alternating refinement of continuum, LSF and lines}

The LSF, the continuum and the line amplitudes constrain one another, and the
problem is not jointly convex in all three, so they are refined alternately.
The fit starts with a pre-fit of the O$_2$ $b$-band shape and a joint solve
of Equation~(\ref{eq:qp}) with all families at the nominal LSF. Five
refinement cycles follow. Each cycle first refits the continuum block with
the line model held fixed, imposing all continuum constraints of
Section~\ref{sec:constraints}; pixels within $\pm2$\,\AA{} of the lines that
carry 99\% of the line flux are down-weighted by $5\times10^{-4}$, and
residual outliers by a Huber weight beyond $3\sigma$. It then refits the LSF
of Equation~(\ref{eq:lsf}) in each channel to the data minus continuum, and
finally re-renders the line templates with the new LSF and refits the line
block. A cycle is kept only if all three stages succeed; otherwise the fit
returns the last kept state, to which the final coefficients, uncertainties
and component spectra refer.

\subsection{Identifiability constraints}
\label{sec:constraints}

The data term alone does not determine several of the partitions the
transfer depends on. Both continuum families are built on the solar spectrum,
so the fit can move flux between moonlight and zodiacal light almost freely:
their \emph{sum} is well constrained, but the \emph{division} is not. We
measure the colour of a component by its log--log slope, the slope of
$\log$ flux against $\log\lambda$. On 200 sky spectra stratified in lunar
phase, the unconstrained fit put the two colours in the wrong order on 164
of 168 moon-up spectra, with a fitted moonlight slope of $+0.21$ where the
physics gives $-3.7$ and a zodiacal slope of $-4.04$ where it gives $-0.3$.
It gave the moon family 45\% of the continuum with the Moon $37^\circ$ below
the horizon, and it produced a ``zodiacal light'' whose amplitude correlated
with lunar illumination at $\rho = +0.95$ and with the tabulated $B_{500}$ at
only $\rho = 0.09$. The split within the diffuse block is not identifiable
either.

All constraints below are hard linear inequalities in the same cone as
$\vect{c}\ge0$; unlike penalties, they never trade against the likelihood.
A constraint \emph{binds} when the solution lies exactly on its bound, and
the quantity it limits is then \emph{pinned}: set by the constraint rather
than measured.

\paragraph{(a) Adjacent-knot ratio bounds.} For each continuum spline family
$f\in\{\rm moon, zodi\}$ and each adjacent pair of coefficients,
\begin{equation}
  \beta\,c^{(f)}_k \;\le\; c^{(f)}_{k+1} \;\le\; \beta^{-1} c^{(f)}_k,
  \qquad \beta = 0.7 .
\end{equation}
This limits how fast a family's multiplier may change with wavelength, so
neither family can take on the colour of the other. With $K$ basis functions
the total permitted swing across the band is $\beta^{-(K-1)}$, so the value
of $\beta$ is specific to the 15 moon and 5 zodiacal basis functions. The
bounds also forbid a spline that falls to zero within the band and
re-emerges, which the unconstrained fit used to lower its residual by
collapsing the moon multiplier across $5100$--$6900$\,\AA. Together with
non-negativity, they make each family either entirely positive or
identically zero.

\paragraph{(b) Moon-share bracket.} With the component integrals
$u = \int T_{\rm moon}\vect{c}_{\rm moon}\,\dd\lambda$ and
$v = \int T_{\rm zodi}\vect{c}_{\rm zodi}\,\dd\lambda$, both linear in
$\vect{c}$, the moonlight fraction is bracketed as
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
  f_{\rm lo}\le\frac{u}{u+v}\le f_{\rm hi}
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\\
  (1-f_{\rm hi})\,u - f_{\rm hi}\,v\le 0,\quad
  f_{\rm lo}\,v - (1-f_{\rm lo})\,u\le 0 .
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
The bracket is centred on $f_{\rm pred}$ (Section~\ref{sec:mzmodel}) with a
multiplicative tolerance $\kappa_f = 3$, and it is clipped to
$[\epsilon, 1-\epsilon]$ with $\epsilon = 0.02$. Constraining the fraction
rather than either amplitude makes the constraint independent of the flux
calibration. The floor $\epsilon$ protects the two regimes where the
prediction is least reliable: the below-horizon taper, which is an
extrapolation, and thin crescents, where the lunar albedo model runs out of
phase coverage.

\paragraph{(c) Absolute zodiacal anchor.} The bracket in (b) is relative: it
can only divide whatever total the two families hold. It cannot prevent both
from growing with the Moon. An absolute anchor does:
\begin{equation}
  \kappa_z^{-1}\,C\,Z_{\rm pred} \;\le\; v \;\le\; \kappa_z\,C\,Z_{\rm pred},
\end{equation}
with $\kappa_z = 2$ and $C = 1.6$. The calibration $C$ recentres the model
on dark time, where the zodiacal light is set by the data; there the median
of $\log_{10}(v/C Z_{\rm pred})$ is $-0.06$. The width $\kappa_z$ matches
the dark-time scatter of that ratio, about 0.15\,dex, so a tighter bracket
would clip real variation. Because $Z_{\rm pred}$ enters with a non-zero
right-hand side, it must be on the flux scale of the data being fitted.

The anchor binds from above on most moon-up observations, and this is
correct. Releasing it to $\kappa_z = 100$ on 294 spectra stratified in lunar
illumination only re-partitions a conserved continuum. The freed zodiacal
light rises by 0.19\,dex while the moonlight falls by 0.11\,dex and the
diffuse block by 0.44\,dex; the total continuum is conserved to 0.5\%, and
the median residual improves by only 0.6\%. The excess follows the Moon, not
the ecliptic: with FLI the fractional lunar illumination, its partial
correlations are $\rho({\rm excess},{\rm FLI}\,|\,B_{500}) = +0.73$ and
$\rho({\rm excess}, B_{500}\,|\,{\rm FLI}) = -0.32$, and the second has the
wrong sign for zodiacal light. The released flux is moonlight leaking into
the zodiacal family, which the anchor prevents.

\paragraph{(d) Diffuse species-ratio bracket.} Without further constraints
the fitted FeO/HO$_2$ flux ratio spreads over 10\,dex, and HO$_2$ is driven
to exactly zero on 23\% of observations. HO$_2$ emits only 5\% of its flux
below 9800\,\AA{} and is a featureless red continuum within the band. This
spread is not information: the three arms of one exposure, which look at the
same airglow a few degrees apart, disagree about the ratio by 0.63\,dex at
the median. Both species ratios are therefore bracketed about the sample's
geometric-median flux shares, which are 0.040, 0.703 and 0.258 for HO$_2$,
FeO and O$_2$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
  \Big|\log_{10}\frac{A_{\rm FeO}}{A_{\rm HO_2}} - \log_{10}\frac{0.703}{0.040}\Big| \le 0.2,\\
  \Big|\log_{10}\frac{A_{\rm O_2}}{A_{\rm HO_2}} - \log_{10}\frac{0.258}{0.040}\Big| \le 0.2 .
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
Each side of a ratio bound is one linear inequality, $A_k - R\,A_0 \le 0$.
Centring on the PALACE reference shares instead would cost 26\% of the blue
$\chi^2$, against 0.7\% for the sample median; this is the site difference of
Section~\ref{sec:diffuse} in numbers. The width costs almost nothing, because
the scatter it removes is degeneracy. On 500 stratified observations the
5--95\% span of the ratio collapses from 10.3 to 0.40\,dex and near-zero
HO$_2$ falls from 23\% to 0.2\% of observations, for $+2.5\%$ in blue
$\chi^2$ and no measurable full-band cost. The constraint moves the split
between the diffuse and zodiacal families but hardly their sum: the change in
$\log_{10}$ of zodi plus diffuse is below 0.012 at the 90th percentile.

\paragraph{(e) Moon-gated cap on the diffuse block.} The diffuse species are
mesospheric chemiluminescence and cannot depend on the Moon. Yet the ratio
$A_{\rm diff}/A_{\rm OH}$ rises with the predicted moonlight fraction at
$\rho = +0.72$, while airmass and OH brightness give $|\rho| < 0.1$. At full
Moon about three quarters of the fitted FeO is moonlight.
Normalising by OH makes the leak measurable, because OH is the
best-determined airglow amplitude and is itself insensitive to the Moon. The
cap is
\begin{equation}
  A_{\rm HO_2} + A_{\rm FeO} + A_{\rm O_2} \;\le\; 10^{\,c_0 + W} A_{\rm OH},
\end{equation}
with $c_0 = -0.649$, the dark-time median of the ratio, and
$W = 0.15$\,dex. $A_{\rm OH}$ is a constant
in the continuum step, because the line model is held fixed there, so the cap
is one inequality with a right-hand side, re-installed in every cycle. The
cap is one-sided, because the leak has one direction. It applies only where
$f_{\rm pred} > 0.6$, because the dark-time scatter of the ratio (0.22\,dex)
is real and an ungated cap costs 15--32\% of the blue $\chi^2$. It caps the
whole block rather than FeO alone, because a cap on FeO alone made the ratio
bracket (d) bind instead and left half of the gated observations above the
bound.

On 1000 random observations the cap binds on 70\% of the gated ones and
leaves none above it. It reduces FeO by 54\% and the block by 42\% there,
sends 98.6\% of the released flux to the moon family and none to the
zodiacal family (which the anchor protects), and conserves the continuum
total to 0.03\%. It costs 1.3\% of the blue $\chi^2$ where it binds and
nothing measurable elsewhere, and it removes 77\% of the moon-attributable
excess of the 580--610\,nm feature. The remaining correlation is
structural: in the faintest quartile of gated moonlight the ratio already
lies below the cap, so a flat ceiling cannot remove it.

\subsection{Retry on reversal}
\label{sec:retry}

A spectrum is \emph{reversed} when its fitted moonlight is redder than its
fitted zodiacal light, $s_{\rm moon} > s_{\rm zodi}$, where $s$ is the
log--log slope of the reconstructed component. The test is applied only
where both families carry flux, $0.05\le u/(u+v)\le0.95$. A reversed spectrum
is mislabelled rather than noisy: its moon coefficients describe zodiacal
light. After constraints (a)--(c), reversals are near-ties (all within 2.0
of the boundary in the separation metric) and occur on 1--5\% of testable
spectra. They are more frequent at low lunar illumination and altitude, and
3.6 times more frequent in the science arm than in the far arm, consistent
with faint-source continuum in the science fibers. Their amplitudes are
nearly right, because the data barely distinguish the two branches
($\rho({\rm moon},{\rm zodi}) = -0.95$). Refitting a reversed spectrum with
a tighter ratio bound, $\beta = 0.85$, recovered all 18 in a test set, for a
median $\chi^2$ increase of 2.8\% and a change in the moonlight fraction only
in the fourth decimal.

The decomposition therefore retries every reversed spectrum at
$\beta = 0.85$ and keeps the retry if it is no longer reversed. The retry
and its outcome are recorded in the flags (Section~\ref{sec:outputs}). The
photon weighting of Section~\ref{sec:noise} moves the partition enough to
raise the first-fit reversal count, and the retry absorbs the increase.

\subsection{Coefficient uncertainties}
\label{sec:uncertainties}

At the optimum the coefficients split into an active set $\mathcal{A}$
($c_k > 0$) and coefficients pinned at zero. On the active set the
regularised Fisher matrix is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  \mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{A}} = {}&\mathbf{A}_{:,\mathcal{A}}^{\!\top}\mathbf{W}
  \mathbf{A}_{:,\mathcal{A}}
  + 2\lambda_{\rm m}\,\mathbf{D}^{(2)\top}\mathbf{D}^{(2)}\big|_{\mathcal{A},\rm moon}\\
  &+ 2\lambda_{\rm z}\,\mathbf{D}^{(2)\top}\mathbf{D}^{(2)}\big|_{\mathcal{A},\rm zodi},
\end{split}
\end{equation}
and the covariance is $\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}$, inflated by
$\max(\chi^2_\nu,1)$ to absorb model misfit. Pinned coefficients have no
defined uncertainty. The marginal errors
$\sigma_k = [\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}]_{kk}^{1/2}$ misrepresent
near-collinear blocks. Adjacent moon-spline coefficients remain correlated at
$|\rho|\approx0.88$, so $\sigma_k$ overstates the uncertainty of each knot
and understates that of joint quantities such as the block amplitude. The
full covariance blocks
$\mathbf{\Sigma}_g = [\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}]_{\mathcal{A}\cap g,\,\mathcal{A}\cap g}$
are therefore also kept for the moon and zodiacal families.

\subsection{Outputs and reliability flags}
\label{sec:outputs}

For each spectrum the decomposition returns the coefficients $\vect{c}$ and
their marginal errors, the moon and zodiacal covariance blocks, the
component spectra (reconstructed with the final LSF and summing to the
model), the fitted LSF and O$_2$ band shape, summary statistics and a
reliability bit mask.

\subsection{Selection of the training sample}
\label{sec:selection}

The transfer is trained only on observations whose coefficients mean what
their names say. An observation is rejected if any of its three spectra
fails one of the following tests:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Field exclusion}: pointings within $10^\circ$ of the Large and
        Small Magellanic Clouds, where bright stellar populations and ionised
        gas at velocities close to the airglow blend with the science-arm
        fit.
  \item \emph{Physical context}: the pointing is above the horizon, the
        airmass is at most 3, and the van Rhijn factors are finite and
        $\ge1$.
  \item \emph{Fit quality}: reduced $\chi^2$ below the 90th percentile of
        the sample.
  \item \emph{Reversal}: moon/zodiacal reversal after the retry.
  \item \emph{Coefficient runaway}: hard limits on FeO and K\,\textsc{i},
        and a robust limit on the mean OH coefficient, which catches
        spectra whose OH block ran away by many orders of magnitude.
  \item \emph{Diffuse collapse}: all three diffuse species below $10^{-3}$
        of their median. The fit put no diffuse continuum at all and let the
        zodiacal family carry it. The distribution is bimodal, with nothing
        between $10^{-6}$ and $10^{-2}$ of the median.
  \item \emph{Science-continuum colour excess}: the red/blue continuum colour
        of the science spectrum exceeds that of the sky arms by more than
        0.05. The fiber carries continuum that the sky model cannot
        represent, and the moon family has absorbed it.
  \item \emph{Outlier clipping}: iterative $\kappa\sigma$ clipping
        ($\kappa = 8$) of the concatenated three-arm coefficient vector, and
        of the OH block with $\kappa = 6$ on a robust scale.
\end{enumerate}
These tests select the training sample only. In production every
observation is predicted, and the flags of Table~\ref{tab:flags} report
what the tests would have concluded.

\section{Geometric normalisation and effective extinction}
\label{sec:geometry}

The three arms of an observation are simultaneous but have different zenith
angles, so each sees a different slant path through the emitting layers and
the atmosphere. This part of the difference between the arms is known
physics, and it is removed in closed form before the transfer so that the
network does not have to learn it from limited data.

\subsection{Scaling law}

For a thin emitting shell at height $h$, the observed amplitude at zenith
angle $z$ and airmass $X$ relates to the zenith-equivalent amplitude by
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
  A_{\rm obs} = A_{\rm int}\;V(z;h)\;10^{-0.4\,k(\lambda)\,[X(z)-1]},\\
  V(z;h) = \left[1-\Big(\frac{R_\oplus}{R_\oplus+h}\Big)^2\sin^2 z\right]^{-1/2},
\end{gathered}
  \label{eq:vanrhijn}
\end{equation}
where $V$ is the van Rhijn factor \citep{vanRhijn1921} and $k$ is the
extinction coefficient. $V$ saturates toward the horizon, at 6.0 for
$h = 87$\,km and 3.4 for $h = 285$\,km, because the observer is inside a
curved shell. A plane-parallel $\sec z$ would overstate the enhancement by
4\% at $z = 60^\circ$ for a mesospheric layer and by 12\% for an ionospheric
one. That error is a smooth function of $z$, so it would not average out.

\subsection{Normalisation and its inverse}
\label{sec:normalisation}

For every airglow coefficient of each of the three spectra,
\begin{equation}
  c^{\rm int}_{r,k} = \frac{c^{\rm obs}_{r,k}}{V(z_r;h_{g(k)})\,10^{-0.4\,k_k\,(X_r-1)}},
\end{equation}
where $g(k)$ is the group of coefficient $k$ and $k_k$ its effective
extinction coefficient. The transfer is trained on these zenith-equivalent
amplitudes and predicts them for the science pointing. The prediction is
then multiplied by the science-pointing factor to restore observed
amplitudes. The ordering matters: the normalisation is applied to physical
coefficients, \emph{before} the non-linear transforms and the robust scaling
of Section~\ref{sec:compression}. Neither of these commutes with a division
by $V$. For example, $(\sqrt{c}-m)/V \neq \sqrt{c/V}-m$, so a correction
applied after the transforms would be wrong at the order of $V$ itself.

Because the reference is $X = 1$ rather than $X = 0$, the normalised
amplitude is zenith-equivalent. It still carries one airmass of extinction,
which cancels in the transfer. It should not be read as a physical layer
emissivity.

\label{sec:heights}

emission and are exempt (Section~\ref{sec:exempt}).

\subsection{Per-coefficient effective wavelength}

Extinction varies strongly across the LVM range. Even within the OH Meinel
system $k(\lambda)$ changes by nearly a factor of two, so a single wavelength
per group is inadequate. Each coefficient $j$ is assigned the
intensity-weighted centroid of its own template,
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_{\rm eff}(j) = \frac{\sum_i \lambda_i\,|T_j(\lambda_i)|}{\sum_i |T_j(\lambda_i)|},
\end{equation}
computed from the design matrix of the decomposition itself. The rotational
and vibrational populations, the LSF and the band blending are therefore
included by construction. Named atomic lines with no support on the grid fall
back to their laboratory wavelengths.

\subsection{Effective extinction}
\label{sec:extinction}

Three facts determine how $k$ should be chosen.

\paragraph{Only differences matter.} The sky-to-science transfer of an
airglow amplitude is
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
  R = \frac{V(z_{\rm sci})}{V(z_{\rm sky})}\,10^{-0.4\,k\,(X_{\rm sci}-X_{\rm sky})},\\
  \frac{\partial\ln R}{\partial k} = -0.4\ln10\,\Delta X \approx -0.92\,\Delta X .
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
The absolute value of $k$ cancels. A per-night error $\delta k$ enters only
as $0.92\,\delta k\,\Delta X$, which is 0.2--0.7\% for typical aerosol
variability ($\delta k\sim0.02$--$0.04$\,mag per airmass) and
$\Delta X\sim0.2$. That is an order of magnitude below the gravity-wave
floor, so per-exposure extinction is not modelled.

\paragraph{A stellar extinction curve is wrong for an extended source.}
For a point source, scattered photons are lost. For a source that fills the
sky they are largely replaced by photons scattered in from neighbouring
lines of sight. Scattering dominates $k$ wherever airglow matters, so the
stellar curve over-corrects, most severely in the blue. This is a coherent
bias, not random scatter.

\paragraph{Height and extinction are degenerate.} For $z\le60^\circ$,
$\ln V(z;h)$ and $X-1$ are collinear to $\rho>0.995$. The data constrain only
the product $V\,10^{-0.4k(X-1)}$, which is all the normalisation needs.

\paragraph{Estimator.} We therefore fit an effective $k_{\rm eff}$ with the
airglow as its own source. For coefficient $j$ in the simultaneous near and
far spectra,
\begin{equation}
  \ln\frac{A_{\rm nr}}{A_{\rm fr}} - \ln\frac{V_{\rm nr}}{V_{\rm fr}}
  = -0.4\ln10\;k_{\rm eff}\,(X_{\rm nr}-X_{\rm fr}) + \varepsilon ,
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon$ is the gravity-wave fluctuation between the two lines of
sight. It has zero mean in the logarithm and is uncorrelated with the
airmass difference. The intrinsic emissivity cancels, because both arms
measure the same coefficient at the same instant. The fitted $k_{\rm eff}$
absorbs the multiple-scattering correction, the site aerosol level and any
error in the assumed layer height. Because of the degeneracy above, the
layer height is fixed and only $k_{\rm eff}$ is fitted. We fit all observations at once; 
an amplitude threshold per observation would select on the outcome, 
since the arm at lower airmass has the smaller van Rhijn factor and passes 
the threshold only when it carries a
positive fluctuation, which correlates the residual with the sign of
$\Delta X$ (in tests this inflated $k_{\rm eff}$ by a factor of 1.8). Errors
are clustered by observation, because all coefficients of an observation
share one fluctuation. Split-half values and the intercept, which should
vanish, are monitored; a significant intercept indicates a throughput
offset between the sky channels. The fitted values are interpolated in wavelength over well-constrained bins.
Elsewhere the generic site curve is used. The result is clipped to
$[0, k_{\rm stellar}]$, since multiple scattering makes the stellar curve an
upper bound.

\subsection{Moonlight and zodiacal light are exempt}
\label{sec:exempt}

Neither moonlight nor zodiacal light is thin-shell emission, and both keep
unit geometry factors. Their transfer between pointings is governed by
scattering geometry and by ecliptic geometry, respectively, which the
network receives as context (Section~\ref{sec:ctx}). For both, the airmass
difference enters extinction only at the percent level.

\section{Learned sky-to-science transfer}
\label{sec:ml}

\subsection{Formulation}

Given the coefficients of the near and far arms of an observation, and the
observing \emph{context} of all three arms (features describing the
pointing, lunar, solar and ecliptic geometry, time and solar activity;
Section~\ref{sec:ctx}), the transfer predicts the science-arm coefficients
$\hat{\vect c}_{\rm sci}$. The predicted sky is
$\hat y_{\rm sci}(\lambda) = \mathbf{A}^{\rm obs}_{\rm sci}\,\hat{\vect c}_{\rm sci}$,
reconstructed with the science spectrum's own LSF and transmission. The
transfer works in the zenith-equivalent frame of
Section~\ref{sec:geometry}, and its target, the \emph{truth}, is the
decomposition of the science spectrum itself.

The coefficients span many decades, so the network does not see them
directly. Each is mapped by an invertible transform to a \emph{score}, a
number of order unity (Section~\ref{sec:compression}). An error measured on
the scores is said to be in \emph{score space}; there every coefficient
counts equally, however little flux it carries. An error measured on the
reconstructed spectra is in \emph{flux space}; there each coefficient counts
in proportion to the flux it contributes, which is what matters for sky
subtraction.

\subsection{Coefficient groups}

The 388 coefficients are divided into six \emph{groups} whose members share
a physical driver (Table~\ref{tab:groups}). The groups follow the emitter
families, except that the atomic lines are split into mesopause (atomic)
and thermospheric (ionospheric) lines and the O$_2$ band joins OH in the
mesospheric group. Each group has its own output \emph{head} (the final
network layers that predict its scores), its own blend weight
(Section~\ref{sec:blend}) and its own loss term. Moonlight and zodiacal
light form separate groups because they depend on disjoint context, lunar
and ecliptic geometry respectively.

\subsection{Per-group transforms}
\label{sec:compression}

Each group block $\vect c_g$ is mapped to a score vector $\vect s_g$ by an
invertible transform fitted on the training observations. The coefficients
are first divided by a robust scale $\vect\rho_g$, the median of the
positive training values in the near arm. An elementwise transform $f_g$
follows: $\operatorname{asinh}$ for the mesospheric group, whose
coefficients span many decades and include lines near zero, and
$\sqrt{\cdot}$ for the other groups. The result $\vect u_g$ is standardised,
and for the moon group rotated onto its principal components,
$\vect s_g = \big((\vect u_g-\vect\mu_g)/\vect\sigma_g\big)\,\mathbf{B}_g$
(for the other groups $\mathbf{B}_g$ is the identity). All 15 moon
components are kept, so the rotation decorrelates the spline coefficients
without reducing their number. Finally the scores of all three arms are
robust-scaled by their training median and interquartile range. The inverse
transform
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\vect c}_g = f_g^{-1}\!\big(\hat{\vect s}_g\,\mathbf{B}_g^{\top}\odot\vect\sigma_g + \vect\mu_g\big)\odot\vect\rho_g
\end{equation}
is clipped to the per-coefficient training range, so that $\sinh$ cannot
amplify an outlying prediction.

\subsection{Observing context}
\label{sec:ctx}

Each pointing is described by 42 context features (Table~\ref{tab:ctx}),
computed for the science pointing and for both sky pointings. Positions of
the Sun and Moon are topocentric at Las Campanas at the exposure mid-time. The
helio-ecliptic longitude uses the geocentric Sun. Three features come from
the physical model of Section~\ref{sec:mzmodel}: the predicted ratio of
moonlight at the science pointing to that at the near pointing (zero with
the Moon down), $\log_{10}Z_{\rm pred}$ and $f_{\rm pred}$. The last two are
exactly the quantities that define the zodiacal anchor and the moon-share
bracket, and so what a head needs to reproduce a pinned amplitude.

Figure~\ref{fig:context-amplitude} shows how the fitted amplitudes vary with
twelve of these features, and why the heads are given different context.
The moonlight is bimodal: it sits at the floor of the moon-share bracket
with the Moon down and rises by three decades with the Moon up, following
lunar altitude, phase and illumination closely. The zodiacal light is
nearly constant within about 0.3\,dex and varies mainly with solar
elongation and ecliptic latitude. OH and the atomic lines brighten toward
low altitude and high airmass, as the van Rhijn factor predicts, and OH
fades during the first half of the night. The ionospheric lines fade by
about an order of magnitude through the night, as the F region recombines
after dusk. The diffuse species vary only weakly, mostly with altitude; the
O$_2$ continuum still rises slightly with lunar phase, a remnant of the
moonlight leak that constraint (e) limits.

\subsection{Architecture}
\label{sec:arch}

The network (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}) follows three principles. The two sky
arms are encoded by the same network with shared weights, so an arm's
encoding does not depend on which telescope produced it. Each group has its
own head. And the zodiacal and diffuse-continuum groups bypass the shared
layers (the \emph{trunk}) through isolated branches that see their physical
drivers directly.
Below, $\vect s^{\rm nr}, \vect s^{\rm fr}\in\mathbb{R}^{388}$ are the
scores of the two sky arms and $\vect x^{\rm nr}, \vect x^{\rm fr},
\vect x^{\rm sci}\in\mathbb{R}^{42}$ the scaled context vectors. All hidden
layers use layer normalisation \citep{Ba2016} and GELU activations
\citep{Hendrycks2016}.

\paragraph{Arm encoder.} Each sky arm is encoded by the same network,
\begin{equation}
  \vect e^{\rm nr} = \mathrm{MLP}_{\rm sky}\big([\vect s^{\rm nr};\vect x^{\rm nr}]\big),
  \qquad
  \vect e^{\rm fr} = \mathrm{MLP}_{\rm sky}\big([\vect s^{\rm fr};\vect x^{\rm fr}]\big),
\end{equation}
with widths $430\to768\to384$.

\paragraph{Context encoder.} The science context is embedded separately,
$\vect e^{\rm ctx} = \mathrm{MLP}_{\rm ctx}(\vect x^{\rm sci})$, with width
96.

\paragraph{Symmetric fusion.} The arm encodings are combined as
\begin{equation}
  \vect z = \big[\tfrac12(\vect e^{\rm nr}+\vect e^{\rm fr});\;
  \vect e^{\rm nr}-\vect e^{\rm fr};\;
  |\vect e^{\rm nr}-\vect e^{\rm fr}|;\;
  \vect e^{\rm ctx}\big]\in\mathbb{R}^{1248}.
\end{equation}
The signed difference gives the direction of an asymmetry between the arms
(for example, which arm is closer to the Moon), and its absolute value gives
a magnitude the trunk can use regardless of orientation. The mean and the
absolute difference are symmetric under exchange of the arms. The signed
difference, like the blend of Section~\ref{sec:blend}, deliberately is not:
the near/far labels carry physical meaning, the angular distance to the
science field. The east/west identity of the telescopes enters only as a
context feature.

\paragraph{Trunk and heads.} A trunk
$\vect h = \mathrm{MLP}_{\rm trunk}(\vect z)$ with widths $320\to160$ feeds
two-layer heads ($160\to192\to n_g$) for the moon, mesospheric, ionospheric
and atomic groups. The heads are linear at the output and predict scores.

\paragraph{Isolated zodiacal branch.} The zodiacal head bypasses the trunk.
It reads the near- and far-arm zodiacal scores and a restricted context set
from all three pointings. The set has 19 features: the zodiacal-geometry
features, the moon-geometry features and the two anchor inputs
(Table~\ref{tab:routing}), giving $19\times3+2\times5 = 67$ inputs. They pass
through a branch of widths $64\to32$ and a linear output of 5 scores. The
trunk's 160-dimensional bottleneck is dominated by moon information.
Bypassing it prevents moon-driven and activity-driven variation from leaking
into the zodiacal prediction and gives the head direct access to the
ecliptic geometry. The moon features are included because, with the Moon
down, the zodiacal family also carries residual continuum whose transfer
depends on the lunar regime.

\paragraph{Isolated continuum branch.} The diffuse-continuum head likewise
bypasses the trunk. It reads 9 moon-scattering features and the two arms'
continuum scores ($9\times3 + 2\times3 = 33$ inputs), through widths
$128\to64$ and a head $64\to64\to3$. It was introduced because the continuum
residuals depended non-linearly on moon geometry (a random-forest regression
of the residual on the context reached $R^2 = 0.70$), which the shared trunk
did not represent.

\paragraph{Additive moon--zodiacal coupling.} Moonlight and zodiacal light
both depend on the joint state of lunar scattering and ecliptic geometry. To
let the two heads share that information without breaking the isolation of
the zodiacal branch, a coupling branch reads the union of their context
features (20 features from the three arms) and the moon and zodiacal scores
of both sky arms, $20\times3 + 2\times(15+5) = 100$ inputs, through widths
$64\to32$. The resulting latent $\vect\ell$ is added to both outputs
through linear projections $P_{\rm moon}\in\mathbb{R}^{15\times32}$ and
$P_{\rm zodi}\in\mathbb{R}^{5\times32}$. The projections are initialised to
zero, so at the start of training the coupled network computes exactly the
uncoupled one. The four other groups receive no coupling term.

\paragraph{Output.} For every group the predicted score is an arm blend plus
the head residual (Section~\ref{sec:blend}):
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  \hat{\vect s}_g = {}&\alpha_g\,\vect s^{\rm nr}_g + (1-\alpha_g)\,\vect s^{\rm fr}_g\\
  &+ \hat{\vect s}^{\,\rm head}_g + \Delta_g,
\end{split}
  \label{eq:output}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta_g = P_g\,\vect\ell$ for the moon and zodiacal groups and
$\Delta_g = 0$ for the others.

\subsection{Arm-blend prior}
\label{sec:blend}

A good first estimate of the science coefficients is an interpolation between
the two sky arms. The blend term of Equation~(\ref{eq:output}) makes that
estimate explicit, and the heads predict a correction on top of it. For the
mesospheric, ionospheric and atomic groups, whose transfer is isotropic on
the LVM baseline, the blend weight $\alpha_g$ is a learned scalar clipped to
$[10^{-3}, 1-10^{-3}]$. For the moon, zodiacal and continuum groups it
depends on the science context,
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_g(\vect x^{\rm sci}) = \sigma\big(\vect w_g\cdot\vect x^{\rm sci}_\alpha + b_g\big),
\end{equation}
where $\vect x^{\rm sci}_\alpha$ contains the horizon step, the ecliptic
latitude and the airmass. The weights $\vect w_g$ start at zero and $b_g$ at
the logit of the initial blend, so training starts from a
context-independent blend. Extending the context-dependent blend to all six
groups degrades the ionospheric group. The blend reads only the science
context, so it can learn how the best mixture depends on conditions, but not
which arm is better placed.

The loss is nearly flat in $\alpha$: the head absorbs the systematic part of
a mis-set blend, but not the arm noise the blend passes through. A wrong
$\alpha$ therefore costs accuracy while generating little gradient, and the
sigmoid damps it further. The blend parameters are therefore trained with a
learning rate 30 times that of the rest of the network, which lets $\alpha$
reach the same optimum from either side of its starting value.

Figure~\ref{fig:blend-alpha} shows how the blend weights evolve during
training. All six favour the near arm. The ionospheric, moon and atomic
weights settle within a few tens of epochs at 0.90--0.96; the continuum
weight settles near 0.87, and the zodiacal weight climbs slowly to the same
value. The mesospheric weight keeps falling for most of the training and
ends near 0.55, close to an equal mix of the two arms. Copying the near arm
is also a better naive predictor than the arm mean for all six groups. A
zodiacal weight near 0.9, rather than the 0.5 a smooth, isotropic emitter
would suggest, is consistent with the zodiacal family also carrying
continuum that is not zodiacal light.

\subsection{Loss}
\label{sec:loss}

The loss mixes flux space and score space. The moon, zodiacal and continuum
groups, whose errors matter in proportion to their brightness, are compared
as reconstructed spectra. The line groups are compared in score space, where
the robust scaling makes residuals comparable across coefficients of very
different amplitude.

\paragraph{Flux-space term.} For $g\in\{\text{moon, zodi, continuum}\}$ the
predicted and target scores are converted back to coefficients by a
differentiable version of the inverse transform of
Section~\ref{sec:compression}, multiplied by the science-arm geometry
factor, and projected onto the group's templates $\mathbf{T}_g$ on the full
native wavelength grid. For observation $r$ the term is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}^{\rm flux}_{g,r} = s_g\,\frac{1}{n_\lambda}\sum_{\lambda}
  w_{r\lambda}\,\big[(\hat{\vect c}_{g,r}-\vect c_{g,r})^{\!\top}\mathbf{T}_g\big]_\lambda^2 ,
\end{equation}
where $w_{r\lambda} = 1/(y_{r\lambda}\,s(\lambda))$ is the photon-noise
weight of the observed science flux (Section~\ref{sec:noise}), with a
variance floor of 5\% of the median and normalised to unit mean for each
observation, and $s_g$ scales the median flux term to the median
score-space term so that the group weights below keep their meaning. Unlike
a score-space loss, this term weights each observation by the brightness of
its target: a given score error with a bright Moon, where the sky
subtraction is most at risk, costs up to $10^5$ times more than in dark
time, in proportion to its effect on the delivered spectrum. Each group is
compared through its own templates only.

\paragraph{Log-amplitude term.} The flux term is absolute and is dominated by
the brightest observations. For the moon group, a scale-free companion penalises
fractional amplitude errors equally at all brightnesses:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}^{\rm amp}_{{\rm moon},r} = \lambda_{\rm amp}\left[\log\frac{\sum_\lambda\hat f_r(\lambda)+\epsilon}{\sum_\lambda f_r(\lambda)+\epsilon}\right]^2,
  \qquad \lambda_{\rm amp} = 5,
\end{equation}
Here $f$ is the moon flux and $\epsilon$ is 5\% of its median training
amplitude, so that very faint moonlight does not dominate the ratio. The
zodiacal group has no such term: its amplitude is pinned by the anchor on
most moon-up observations, so an amplitude penalty would only trade
spectral shape for an amplitude the network already reproduces.

\paragraph{Score-space term.} For the mesospheric, ionospheric and atomic
groups,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}^{\rm score}_{g,r} = \frac{1}{n_g}\sum_{k=1}^{n_g} w^{\rm pe}_{g,k,r}\;
  \mathrm{Huber}\big(\hat s_{g,k,r}-s_{g,k,r}\big),
\end{equation}
Here $\mathrm{Huber}$ is quadratic for residuals below one (in scaled units)
and linear beyond. The per-element weights $w^{\rm pe} = 1/\sigma_s^2$
propagate the decomposition uncertainties (Section~\ref{sec:uncertainties})
through the transform to first order,
$\sigma_{s,k} = \lVert\nabla_{\vect c_g} s_k\rVert\,\sigma_{\vect c_g}$,
divided by the robust score scale. The score uncertainty $\sigma_s$ has a
floor at 5\% of its median for each coefficient (20\% for the mesospheric
and atomic groups, whose uncertainties have tails spanning three to six
decades), and the weights are normalised to unit mean over the training
observations.

\paragraph{Total.} The total loss is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{6}\sum_g \frac{m_g}{\sqrt{n_g}}\;
  \Big\langle \mathcal{L}_{g,r}\Big\rangle_r ,
\end{equation}
with group weights $m_{\rm moon} = 3$, $m_{\rm zodi} = 2$ and $m_g = 1$
otherwise, and $\mathcal{L}_{g,r}$ the flux (plus amplitude) term or the
score term as appropriate. Observations are weighted uniformly; reweighting
by regime was tested and did not help.

\subsection{Constraint-derived amplitudes}
\label{sec:rules}

In each half of the sample one continuum amplitude is fixed by a constraint
of the decomposition rather than by the data (Table~\ref{tab:occupancy}).
There the target is a known function of geometry, so it is derived at
prediction time instead of learned. Both rules rescale a whole coefficient
block and leave the predicted spectral shape unchanged. Both are idempotent,
and both are linear in the coefficients, so the ensemble mean of members
that satisfy them satisfies them too. The zodiacal rule is applied first,
because the moonlight rule reads the predicted zodiacal amplitude.

\paragraph{Bright Moon: the zodiacal ceiling.} Where the anchor binds, the
zodiacal amplitude equals $\kappa_z C Z_{\rm pred}$ exactly. With a
constant $S$ fitted on the training observations, the rule clips the
predicted zodiacal amplitude to $S Z_{\rm pred}$ everywhere. This is a hard
upper bound on the fitted value, exceeded by 0.01\% of observations and
never by more than 0.002\,dex, so clipping can only move a prediction toward
the truth. Where $f_{\rm pred} > 0.6$, the rule also sets the amplitude to
the ceiling if the network already predicts at least 90\% of it. This second
condition leaves alone the observations that are genuinely below the
ceiling, which the network places well below it. The loss is unchanged: the
network learns the ceiling, and its own prediction separates pinned from
unpinned observations.

\paragraph{Dark time: the moonlight floor.} Where the moon-share bracket has
collapsed onto its floor, the moonlight is not measured
(Section~\ref{sec:constraints}). In training, the flux loss is made blind to
the amplitude on these observations: the prediction is rescaled to the true
amplitude before the flux term (with the scale factor kept in the gradient,
so that the amplitude gradient vanishes identically), and the
log-amplitude term is dropped, so only the shape is trained. At prediction,
the amplitude is restored from the near arm's measured
moonlight-to-zodiacal ratio,
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  \textstyle\int{\rm moon}_{\rm sci} = {}&\mathrm{clip}\big(
  (\textstyle\int{\rm moon}/\!\int{\rm zodi})_{\rm nr},\,0,\,R\big)\\
  &\times\textstyle\int{\rm zodi}_{\rm sci},
\end{split}
\end{equation}
with the fitted constant $R\approx\epsilon/(1-\epsilon)$ as a fallback when
the near arm is unusable. The ratio is transferred, rather than set to $R$,
because the dark-time moon share is bimodal: 96\% of these observations sit
on the floor and about 3\% want no moonlight at all.

The regime is selected by $f_{\rm pred}\le\epsilon/\kappa_f$, not by lunar
altitude. Scattered moonlight with the Moon a few degrees below the horizon
is real, and the horizon taper multiplies phase and separation: a thin
crescent $2^\circ$ down contributes nothing, while a full Moon $5^\circ$ down
still does. No fixed altitude cut matches this gate in both coverage and
exactness (Table~\ref{tab:moongate}).

\paragraph{Degenerate continuum.} The dark-time observations that want no
moonlight are failed fits rather than a physical state. Their median
$\chi^2$ is 15 times that of other dark-time observations, and all of their
continuum is in the zodiacal family. They are flagged at prediction time
from the two sky arms alone, when both have a moonlight-to-zodiacal ratio
below $R/2$. The flag has 88\% precision at 95\% recall, affects 1.9\% of
observations, and never fires with the Moon up.

\subsection{Bias calibration}
\label{sec:calibration}

After training, a multiplicative bias correction is fitted on the training
and validation observations and applied at prediction time. For the moon and
zodiacal groups it is one factor per coefficient,
$\ell_k = \bar c^{\rm true}_k/\bar c^{\rm pred}_k$, clipped to $[0.7,1.4]$,
because a single factor cannot remove the spectral tilt of the bias. The
moon correction is fitted only where the network, not the dark-time rule,
predicts the amplitude, and the zodiacal correction is fitted separately in
three lunar-altitude regimes with smooth transitions between them. For the
other groups a single factor per group is fitted on the amplitude
(Equation~\ref{eq:amplitude}), clipped to $[0.5, 2]$. For a group whose
templates carry very different flux, the unweighted mean coefficient is a
different quantity from the amplitude, and the two can disagree in sign:
across the 357 OH templates the integrals span nine decades and are
anti-correlated with coefficient size.

\subsection{Training}
\label{sec:training}

Observations are grouped by night and assigned to training, validation and
test sets, stratified by lunar phase. Whole nights are kept together because
the sky of neighbouring exposures within a night is correlated, and a random
split would leak. We call the validation and test observations together the
\emph{held-out} observations: none of them is used to fit network weights.

Each network is trained with AdamW \citep{Loshchilov2019}, with learning
rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $2\times10^{-4}$, gradient clipping at 1 and
batches of 512, for up to 400 epochs with early stopping on the validation
loss and a patience of 100 epochs. The blend parameters have no weight decay
and the raised learning rate of Section~\ref{sec:blend}. The prediction is
the mean of an ensemble of ten networks trained with different random seeds
on the same split, and the ensemble spread is reported as an uncertainty.

Figure~\ref{fig:training-loss} shows the training and validation loss of
each member. The training loss falls by more than an order of magnitude and
is still falling slowly at 400 epochs. The validation loss flattens after
about 100 epochs at roughly twenty times the training loss, and its minimum
is reached anywhere between about 90 and 400 epochs depending on the member;
the brief spikes shared by both curves are single unstable epochs, from
which training recovers.

\subsection{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

The deliverable is a sky spectrum, so the transfer is judged in flux space,
on reconstructed spectra. Coefficient errors can mislead: score-space
metrics are dominated by the 358 mesospheric coefficients, and they can
disagree in sign with flux-space metrics on the same predictions. The
residual $y_{\rm sci} - \hat y_{\rm sci}$ of the predicted sky is compared
with three references. The first is the decomposition's own fit to
$y_{\rm sci}$, which is the floor: the transfer predicts the coefficients of
that fit, so it cannot describe the spectrum better. The second is the
photon noise of a single fiber (Section~\ref{sec:noise}), the level below
which a sky error no longer matters. The third is the sky itself. All
numbers below are for the 2888 held-out observations.

Figure~\ref{fig:chi2-heldout} compares the prediction with the first two
references. Over the full band the median $\chi^2$ of the prediction is
1.27 against the single-fiber noise, and that of the decomposition's own fit
is 0.92, a ratio of 1.38. In the blue the medians are 0.87 and 0.68, a ratio
of 1.28. The typical residual of the predicted sky is therefore 1.13 times
the single-fiber noise per pixel over the full band and 0.93 times it in the
blue, where the decomposition alone reaches 0.96 and 0.82. If the transfer
error adds in quadrature to the decomposition residual, it amounts to about
0.6 of the single-fiber noise over the full band and 0.44 in the blue, or
about 5\% and 4\% of the sky per pixel. About a third of the observations
have a prediction $\chi^2$ below 1 over the full band, and half in the blue.

The upper panels of Figure~\ref{fig:chi2-heldout} show that both
distributions peak below $\chi^2=1$, and that the prediction's is shifted
to higher values with a longer tail. The lower panels pair the two numbers
observation by observation. Most observations lie just above the diagonal,
at a median ratio of 1.23 over the full band and 1.10 in the blue: the
prediction stays close to the floor whatever the absolute $\chi^2$. The
observations well above the diagonal are transfer failures, the only kind
the transfer can reduce; 13\% of observations exceed twice their
decomposition $\chi^2$ over the full band, and 9\% in the blue. The
observations far to the right are those the decomposition itself fits
poorly, so their truth is not reliable either.

Figure~\ref{fig:residual-heldout} shows where in wavelength the error lies,
now relative to the sky. The top panel is the sky-subtracted science
spectrum. Its large spikes, at H$\alpha$, H$\beta$, [O\,\textsc{iii}] and
[O\,\textsc{ii}], are the nebular emission of the science fields that sky
subtraction exists to reveal, not sky residual. Away from them, the
residual scatters around zero with the largest excursions at the brightest
OH lines and in the far blue, and the median residual of an observation is
typically 1.8\% of its sky level (5.5\% at the 84th percentile). Integrated
over the band, the predicted sky matches the observed flux to 1.5\% at the
median, 3.7\% at the 84th percentile. The three lower panels isolate the
transfer by comparing the predicted continuum components with the
decomposition of the science spectrum. The moonlight error dominates: it
grows toward the blue, where scattered moonlight is brightest, and reaches
$\pm1$ in these units near 4000\,\AA, where the bright-Moon continuum is
about 5. The zodiacal and diffuse errors stay within about $\pm0.3$ and
$\pm0.2$, respectively. For all three components the median error of an
observation is narrowly distributed around zero, within or near the
single-fiber noise.

Figure~\ref{fig:atlas} condenses the comparison into percentile bands at
each wavelength, separately for the Moon down (1090 observations,
$f_{\rm pred}\le1/150$) and the Moon up (1798). Here the whole predicted sky
is compared with the full decomposition of the science spectrum. The
residual is therefore the transfer error alone: it contains neither the
emission of the science field nor the residual of the decomposition's own
fit. The median is consistent with zero at every wavelength. Its magnitude
is typically 3\% of the single-fiber noise with the Moon down and 2\% with
the Moon up, and nowhere more than 26\% and 38\%. As a fraction of the sky
it is about 0.2\% in each of the blue, red and near-infrared ranges, with
the prediction slightly high. The 68\% range lies inside the single-fiber
noise on 97\% of pixels in both regimes, so for a typical observation the
transfer error is below the photon noise of one fiber almost everywhere.
The exceptions are the bright lines: [O\,\textsc{i}]\,5577 and 6300,
Na\,\textsc{i}\,D and the OH bands beyond 7300\,\AA, where the line flux far
exceeds the continuum and a small fractional error in the line amplitude is
still large in flux. The 95\% range extends past the noise. With the Moon up
it follows the moonlight, widening toward the blue. With the Moon down it is
skewed to positive values, so the largest errors in dark time are
under-predictions of the sky.

Two cautions apply when reading such numbers. First, the truth carries its
own noise. A useful reference is the disagreement between the two sky arms,
$\Delta = \tfrac12\,\mathrm{rms}(\vect c^{\rm nr}-\vect c^{\rm fr})$, which
is $0.71\sigma$ if each arm carries independent noise $\sigma$. A perfect
predictor still scores an error $\sigma$ against a truth as noisy as the
arms, so the floor for such a group is ${\rm err}/\Delta\approx\sqrt2$, not
1; values well below 1 mean that the head uses context the arms do not
carry. Second, the selection of Section~\ref{sec:selection} does not run in
production. A change aimed at pathological observations can look neutral on
the selected test set, because the selection has already removed most of
its targets, and a pathology that is rare in training need not be rare in
delivery. Changes are therefore measured on the population whose behaviour
they are meant to change, and what cannot be fixed is flagged.

\subsection{Prediction}

At prediction time the inputs are the near and far spectra of an exposure,
their decompositions, and the context of all three arms. The sky
coefficients are normalised (Section~\ref{sec:normalisation}) and
transformed to scores, each ensemble member predicts the science scores
with Equation~(\ref{eq:output}), and the scores are inverted to coefficients
and multiplied by the science-arm geometry factors. The rules of
Section~\ref{sec:rules} and the calibration of Section~\ref{sec:calibration}
are applied, the members are averaged, and the science sky spectrum is
reconstructed with the science spectrum's LSF and transmission.

Each prediction carries a reliability mask in the vocabulary of
Table~\ref{tab:flags}: diffuse collapse is evaluated from the predicted
coefficients, moon/zodiacal reversal from the reconstructed continua, and
the degenerate-continuum flag from the sky arms. The constraint warnings do
not apply to a prediction; they describe how the decomposition shaped the
training targets.

Figures~\ref{fig:pipeline-moon} and~\ref{fig:pipeline-zodi} follow two
observations through the whole procedure: the decompositions of the two sky
arms, the predicted science sky with its components, and the delivered
sky-subtracted spectrum. In the first, with the Moon up, moonlight dominates
the continuum in both arms, and the two arms and the prediction look alike.
The predicted sky follows the observed science spectrum closely: the
residual stays near zero across the band, with its largest excursions in the
far blue and among the strongest OH lines beyond 9000\,\AA, the same places
where the decomposition itself leaves residuals
(Figure~\ref{fig:example-decomp-up}). The second observation was chosen to
test the transfer on a large difference between the arms. The Moon is far
below the horizon ($h_{\rm moon}=-32.9^\circ$, illumination 0.01), so the
moonlight of both arms sits at the floor of the moon-share bracket, and the
difference between the arms comes from zodiacal light, whose brightness
depends on ecliptic latitude. The near and far arms differ by 0.36\,dex (a
factor of 2.3) in median flux and by 0.49\,dex (a factor of 3.1) in
zodiacal-light amplitude, so the transfer has to decide from context alone
how much of each to carry into the science pointing. The residual is again
flat in the continuum. Its many spikes are the nebular emission of this
science field, which the sky subtraction reveals: the Balmer lines,
[O\,\textsc{ii}], [O\,\textsc{iii}], [N\,\textsc{ii}], [S\,\textsc{ii}] and
[S\,\textsc{iii}] 9069, 9531. The decomposition also finds this science
spectrum harder to fit than the first, with a fit $\chi^2$ about six times
higher.
